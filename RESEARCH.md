# Wave 1 — Strategy, Universe, Sizing & Execution Evidence

## Synthesis

### Top insights

- The chosen universe is the single biggest problem, bigger than any strategy detail: volatile sub-$20/sub-$5 stocks are precisely the Kumar (2009) lottery / Bali-Cakici-Whitelaw MAX / high-IVOL cohort with documented NEGATIVE risk-adjusted returns of >1%/month (>12%/yr) versus the market. Academic momentum studies deliberately EXCLUDE sub-$5 stocks to improve returns. Both bots are long-only in the historically worst-performing corner of US equities, so any timing signal must first overcome ~12%/yr of adverse drift before showing an edge.
- The 5-minute trend bot trades against the measured sign of the effect: the closest published test (Marshall, Cahan & Cahan 2008 — 7,846 intraday rules incl. MA crossovers on 5-min SPY bars) found ZERO rules profitable after data-snooping control, BEFORE costs; and at 5-15 minute horizons individual-stock returns are reversal-dominated, not trending (Heston-Korajczyk-Sadka 2010). Continuous 5-min trend-following on single stocks has no published support and the documented autocorrelation runs the wrong way.
- Cost arithmetic makes the sub-$5 leg structurally expensive regardless of signal quality: the Reg NMS 1-cent tick floor means a $2-3 stock has a 33-50 bps minimum quoted spread; IBKR tiered per-share commissions add ~14 bps/side at $2.50 with $2k clips; Schwarz et al. (JoF 2025) measured IBKR as the WORST tested broker for small market orders (~0.44% round trip). Each round trip consumes roughly 15-35% of the 3% stop budget before any adverse move — the strategy can lose to friction alone even with a zero-edge-neutral signal.
- IBKR paper trading + 15-minute delayed data means all P&L from the 5-min bot is an upper bound with near-zero live predictive power: IBKR documents paper fills as top-of-book-only simulations (users report better-than-limit fills via API), delayed quotes make 5-min signals ~3 bars stale, and Wiecki et al. (2016, 888 real strategies) found backtest Sharpe predicts out-of-sample performance with R-squared < 0.025. A positive paper result should trigger a fill-artifact audit, not confidence.
- The two bots sit on opposite sides of the best-documented structural divide in equity returns: since 1993 essentially the ENTIRE equity premium accrued overnight while open-to-close returns were ~zero-to-negative (Cooper-Cliff-Gulen 2008; Lou-Polk-Skouras 2019). Flatten-at-close puts the intraday bot permanently on the losing side of that split — though in this dilution-prone universe it also genuinely protects against overnight offering/reverse-split gap-downs, so it's a measurable trade-off, not a free lunch either way. The daily bot holds overnight (right side of the premium) but therefore bears full gap/dilution risk: its true worst-case loss is 2-3x the nominal stop.
- The flat 3% stop on 5-minute bars is in the regime where stops provably destroy expected value: Kaminski-Lo (2014) show stop-losses add value only under positive serial correlation and ALWAYS reduce expected return under a random walk (and actively hurt under mean reversion — the documented sub-hour regime). Sub-$5 high-volume names routinely move 3% within minutes, so this stop is a noise-triggered loss-realization machine, and stop-market orders fill through the trigger by at least the spread plus slippage.
- The SMA-crossover entry has adverse selection built in for this universe: a crossover on a volatile cheap stock typically fires right after outlier up-days, i.e., it systematically buys the high-MAX-month cohort with the worst forward returns (>1%/month underperformance); simultaneously, sub-$5 issuers sell into exactly those spikes via ATMs/PIPEs (death-spiral convert issuers earn ~-1.8%/month after), truncating the very trends the bot needs. Trend persistence validated on liquid large caps does not transfer here.
- What the trend-following literature actually validates is slow and broad, not this: 3-12 month lookbacks diversified across 50+ uncorrelated futures markets, with per-market Sharpe only ~0.4 and decade-long droughts (SG Trend: <2%/yr with 22% drawdown 2009-2018). The Sharpe surface across lookback choices is nearly flat, so optimizing the SMA pair on a handful of tickers is pure data snooping — the exact failure mode that killed the Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron results out-of-sample. A daily bot on 3-5 names generates too few independent signals for a year of results to mean anything.
- Position sizing is the one lever with robust, replicated evidence: fixed-fractional risk-based sizing (0.5-1% of current equity per trade) with ATR-scaled stop distances is the per-trade form of inverse-vol scaling, whose replicable benefit is tail/drawdown reduction, not extra return (Harvey et al. 2018; Cederburg et al. 2020 showed the return-boost claims failed out-of-sample). Ruin math converges on the same range: ~18% 95th-percentile drawdown at 1% risk vs 60%+ at 5%.
- Capped limit entries are directionally right (Anand et al. 2025: limits save ~10 bps on average, MORE in wide-spread volatile small-caps) but create a measurement obligation: passive limits fill disproportionately when the move is failing and miss the winners, so fill-price savings without counterfactual tracking of unfilled signals overstate the benefit — for a trend system the missed fills are systematically the profitable ones.
- Base rates define the honest success criterion: 97% of Brazilians who day-traded 300+ sessions lost money, <1% of Taiwanese day traders reliably beat fees, and no published simple intraday technical rule on individual stocks survives retail costs. For an educational system, the research-consistent win condition is 'losses fully explained by measured costs, staleness, and time-of-day effects' — not positive P&L.

### Concrete upgrades the evidence supports

- **Risk-based ATR position sizing on both bots** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Replace dollar-based sizing with shares = (current equity x 0.5-1%) / stop distance, where stop distance = 2-3x ATR(14) daily bars for the daily bot and 1.5-2x ATR(14) of 5-min bars for the intraday bot. For the daily bot, size for ~0.4-0.5% nominal risk so a 2-3x overnight gap still caps true worst-case near 1%. Risk as % of current equity auto-deleverages in drawdowns.
  - Evidence: Strongest-evidenced item across all five reports: Moreira-Muir 2017 / Harvey et al. 2018 inverse-vol scaling (robust tail reduction), Barroso-Santa-Clara 2015 (momentum Sharpe 0.53->0.97, kurtosis 18->2.7), fractional-Kelly estimation-error math, and ruin percentiles (18% max-DD at 1% risk vs 60%+ at 5%).
- **Replace the flat 3% stop with an ATR-scaled stop (or reframe it as catastrophe cap)** *(effort: small)*
  - What: On the 5-min bot, set stops at 1.5-2x ATR(14) of 5-min bars so the stop sits outside one bar of routine noise; if 3% must stay, explicitly treat it as a catastrophe cap and size positions on that assumption. Backtest/paper-account every stop fill at stop price MINUS one full spread minus a 10-50 bps slippage haircut, never at the trigger price.
  - Evidence: Kaminski-Lo 2014: stops reduce expected return under random-walk/mean-reverting returns (the documented 5-15 min regime); Han-Zhou-Zhu show stops help trend systems only when set outside noise; execution report: stop-market slippage through trigger is unbounded and unmeasured for retail — must be modeled pessimistically.
- **Execution-quality instrumentation loop (the real educational deliverable)** *(effort: medium)*
  - What: For every order log: NBBO/delayed quote at decision time, signal price, limit price, fill/no-fill, fill price, time-to-fill, spread paid, and slippage vs signal. For every UNFILLED signal, log counterfactual P&L of the missed trade. Report monthly: effective spread, implementation shortfall, cost-per-round-trip as % of the stop budget, and paper-vs-theoretical fill discrepancy.
  - Evidence: Three reports independently converge on this: no credible published live-vs-backtest slippage numbers exist for retail systems (genuine evidence gap — own fill log is the only calibration source); Anand et al. adverse-selection of passive limit fills; IBKR paper-fill optimism documented by IBKR itself.
- **Conservative paper-fill convention** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Count a resting limit as filled only if price trades THROUGH the limit by >= 1 tick (touch is not a fill); add commission + half-spread per side on top of every simulated fill; haircut any resulting Sharpe by ~50% before drawing conclusions.
  - Evidence: IBKR documents top-of-book-only paper fills; Elite Trader reports of better-than-limit API paper fills; Wiecki et al. R-squared < 0.025 backtest-to-live; McLean-Pontiff 26-58% post-publication decay supporting the standard ~50% haircut heuristic.
- **Time-of-day filters and MOC exit** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Block new 5-min-bot entries in the first 15-30 minutes (widest spreads, high-open fade); add a spread filter skipping trades when quoted spread > ~50 bps (requires live quotes — see next item); replace the 3:55-3:59 flatten with a Market-on-Close order into the closing auction.
  - Evidence: Robust U-shape microstructure literature (spreads widest at open); Cooper et al. high-open fade in the first hour; closing auction documented as the deepest liquidity event of the day for small names.
- **Live quote subscription for the intraday bot (or kill the intraday bot's live pretensions)** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Subscribe to one real-time exchange feed (cheap on IBKR) so 5-min signals aren't computed on 15-min-delayed data (3 bars stale) and the spread filter is possible. Alternatively, explicitly document that the delayed-data bot is a cost-measurement exercise whose signals are stale by construction.
  - Evidence: Delayed data makes every 5-min signal 3 bars old — directly flagged in the intraday and execution reports as making paper P&L an upper bound; a spread filter is impossible without seeing the live spread.
- **Universe hygiene filters for the daily bot** *(effort: medium)*
  - What: Exclude names with: a reverse split in the past 1-2 years (-10.8% 1-yr, -33.9% 3-yr abnormal returns), active S-3 shelf/ATM or rapid share-count growth, going-concern language; skip entries when the prior month's max daily return or prior 5-day gain is in the universe's top decile (avoid buying the high-MAX cohort). Never extend to OTC/pink sheets (measured retail outcomes -8% to -37%/yr).
  - Evidence: Desai-Jain 1997 and Kim-Klein-Rosenfeld 2008 reverse-split evidence; Hillion-Vermaelen death-spiral financing (-1.8%/month); Bali et al. MAX effect (>1%/month underperformance of top decile); SEC DERA OTC white paper (median -11.9% per trade).
- **Control-group benchmarks to isolate the universe cost** *(effort: medium)*
  - What: Run the identical 5-min logic on SPY or QQQ in parallel as a control; run a slow (3-12 month) trend/absolute-momentum sleeve on 5-10 liquid uncorrelated ETFs as the daily bot's benchmark. The performance gaps directly measure (a) the microstructure tax of cheap stocks and (b) the value of breadth — the two central lessons in the literature.
  - Evidence: Marshall et al. tested on SPY (so the control matches the published null); Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen / Hurst et al. show trend profits come from breadth across uncorrelated markets (per-market Sharpe ~0.4), not single-name signals.
- **Pre-registered parameters and evaluation protocol** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Fix one SMA pair (or an average of 2-3 speeds) in writing before further trading and never change it based on recent P&L; evaluate on median trade P&L, hit rate, drawdown, and cost decomposition — not mean return — and only after 300+ logged round trips. Never compute a Kelly fraction from paper results (win-rate SE is ~+/-5pp at 100 trades).
  - Evidence: Flat Sharpe surface across lookbacks (Levine-Pedersen equivalence) makes pair-picking pure snooping — the documented cause of the BLL out-of-sample collapse; lottery-shaped return distributions make means misleading; MacLean-Thorp-Ziemba estimation-error asymmetry.
- **Portfolio heat cap plus simple drawdown ratchet** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Cap summed open risk at 4-6% of equity across all concurrent positions (these names crash together); cap gross exposure at ~100% of the $20k, scaling toward 50% when portfolio realized vol enters its top decile; halve per-trade risk% whenever equity is >10% below high-water mark, restore at new highs. Weight concurrent positions inverse to ATR%-of-price rather than equal dollars.
  - Evidence: Conditional vol targeting beats continuous (Bongaerts et al. 2020 FAJ); risk-control-index and naive-risk-parity results; fixed-fractional ruin math. Practitioner-grade but cheap, mechanical, and aligned with the academic tail-reduction findings.
- **Order-type upgrade: never naked market orders on IBKR** *(effort: small)*
  - What: Use marketable limits capped at NBBO ask (or ask + 1 tick) or IBKR Midprice orders for entries (saves ~16 bps/side on a tick-constrained $3 stock); A/B test the Adaptive algo (Patient/Normal) against plain capped limits using the fill log before trusting IBKR's self-reported claims. Prefer the $5-$20 leg for capital; on sub-$5 names size orders >100 shares so the $0.35 tiered minimum doesn't inflate the rate.
  - Evidence: Schwarz et al. JoF 2025: IBKR small market orders worst-of-breed at ~0.44% round trip; Anand et al. limit-order savings largest in this exact universe; IBKR Adaptive superiority is vendor marketing with no independent verification.
- **Re-run cost model at the November 2026 half-penny tick change** *(effort: small)*
  - What: When the SEC's half-penny tick regime for tick-constrained stocks goes live (first business day of Nov 2026 after exemptive relief), re-estimate minimum spreads for the sub-$5 universe — it should roughly halve the tick-floor spread cost on high-volume tick-constrained names.
  - Evidence: SEC Reg NMS Minimum Pricing Increments final rule, upheld by D.C. Circuit Oct 2025, implementation delayed to Nov 2026 — cited in the execution report.

### Warnings: where the evidence contradicts current practice

- The universe choice contradicts the evidence more than any other decision: both bots go long-only in the lottery-stock cohort with >1%/month documented risk-adjusted UNDERPERFORMANCE, which quant funds explicitly exclude to improve returns. The system is not fishing in a neutral pond with a maybe-edge; it is fishing in a pond with a measured negative drift, and no published result suggests a daily SMA crossover overcomes that headwind.
- The 5-minute trend bot contradicts the published sign of the effect: intraday single-stock returns mean-revert at 5-15 minute horizons, and the direct test of thousands of 5-min technical rules (including MA crossovers) found zero value before costs. Expect this bot to lose steadily to spread + whipsaw + adverse fills; the research-consistent outcome is a well-documented loss, and the write-up should say so up front rather than framing it as an alpha attempt.
- The flat 3% stop on 5-min bars is evidence-contradicted twice over: it sits inside one bar of routine noise for sub-$5 movers (guaranteeing frequent stop-outs), and Kaminski-Lo prove stops in random-walk/mean-reverting regimes are pure expected-return drag. As currently configured, the stop IS the strategy's dominant exit and its dominant cost.
- Do not trust any positive paper P&L without a fill audit: IBKR paper fills are top-of-book simulations that can fill better than limit, delayed data makes 5-min signals 3 bars stale, and backtest Sharpe has near-zero (R-squared < 0.025) predictive power for live results. The current setup will systematically flatter the intraday bot in exactly the ways the literature warns about.
- Flatten-at-close structurally forfeits the entire equity premium window (overnight), while the daily bot's overnight holding means its real worst-case loss is 2-3x the nominal stop due to gaps from offerings, halts, and reverse splits — the current risk accounting understates the daily bot's tail if it assumes the stop caps losses.
- Any temptation to optimize the SMA pair, add the best-backtested lookback, or compute Kelly sizing from paper results is the documented failure mode: the lookback Sharpe surface is flat (differences on a few tickers are noise), post-publication decay averages 26-58%, and Kelly estimates from <300 trades are noise with asymmetric over-betting penalties.
- If the ORB/'Stocks in Play' literature ever motivates a redesign, note that the headline numbers (Sharpe 2.81, 36% alpha) come from non-peer-reviewed authors who sell trading education, depend on leverage and optimistic fills, and independent replications show decay — expect breakeven-at-best after realistic sub-$5 costs.
- IBKR-specific: naked market orders are measured as the most expensive way to trade small size at this broker (~0.44% round trip, worst of five brokers tested) — if any part of the system currently sends market orders (including stop-markets and the pre-close flatten), that choice is directly contradicted by the execution evidence.
- Never extend the universe to OTC/pink sheets under any circumstances: measured retail outcomes there are -8% to -37% per year with heavy promotion/manipulation — categorically worse than the already-adverse listed sub-$5 segment.

## Intraday equity strategy evidence: 5-minute technical rules, ORB, VWAP, time-of-day effects, and overnight-vs-intraday return decomposition

### Key findings

- MOST DIRECT TEST OF 5-MIN TECHNICAL RULES IS NEGATIVE: Marshall, Cahan & Cahan (2008, Journal of Empirical Finance, 'Does intraday technical analysis in the U.S. equity market have value?') tested 7,846 popular intraday technical rules (filter rules, moving-average crossovers, support/resistance, channel breakouts) on 5-minute SPDR (SPY) bars in 2002 (bear) and 2003 (bull), using both a Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron bootstrap and White's Reality Check to control data snooping. Result: NO rule was profitable after data-snooping adjustment in either market regime. This is the closest published analogue to a 5-minute-bar MA trend bot, and it finds zero value even before retail-level costs.
- DAILY-BAR TECHNICAL RULES ALSO FAIL MODERN DATA AFTER SNOOPING/COSTS: Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999) applied the Reality Check to 7,846 rules on the DJIA and found the apparent historical edge of the best rules vanished out-of-sample after ~1986. Bajgrowicz & Scaillet (2012, JFE) revisited DJIA 1897-2008 with a false-discovery-rate approach and concluded a real-time investor could never have profitably exploited the rules once transaction costs were included, and performance disappeared entirely in recent decades. Genetic-programming searches over intraday rules similarly find no excess returns once realistic transaction costs and trading hours are imposed.
- OPENING RANGE BREAKOUT IS THE ONLY INTRADAY TEMPLATE WITH RECENT POSITIVE CLAIMS, BUT THE EVIDENCE IS PRACTITIONER-GRADE, NOT PEER-REVIEWED: An academic study of 'timely' ORB on five index futures markets reports >8% annual returns (p<0.03 in all five markets, best 20.28%/yr on TAIEX). Zarattini & Aziz (SSRN 2023, 'Can Day Trading Really Be Profitable?') report a 5-min ORB on QQQ/TQQQ 2016-2023 with 10R profit targets returning ~1,485% total on TQQQ vs 169% buy-and-hold QQQ; Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz (SSRN 2024) report an ORB on top-20 'Stocks in Play' (high relative volume) with >1,600% net, Sharpe 2.81, ~36% annualized alpha. Major caveats: both authors run trading-education/research businesses (Bear Bull Traders, Concretum Group), papers are not peer-reviewed, results depend on leverage, fill assumptions and relative-volume stock selection; independent backtests (QuantifiedStrategies and others) find simple ORB on S&P index products no longer consistently profitable and attribute decay to crowding.
- MARKET INTRADAY MOMENTUM IS THE BEST-DOCUMENTED PEER-REVIEWED INTRADAY EFFECT, AND IT IS SMALL: Gao, Han, Li & Zhou (JFE 2018, 'Market Intraday Momentum') show on SPY 1993-2013 that the first half-hour return (measured from prior close, i.e., including overnight) positively predicts the last half-hour return: slope significant at 1%, R-squared ~1.6% (2.6% adding the 12th half-hour). Effect is stronger on high-volatility, high-volume, recession, and macro-news days, and holds on 10 other liquid ETFs. But post-publication evidence is mixed: at least one out-of-sample study finds the predictability disappears in later samples or becomes regime-dependent (signal-strength/Markov-switching conditioning needed). Note this is an index/ETF effect at specific half-hours - not a license for continuous 5-minute trend-following in single stocks.
- CROSS-SECTIONAL INTRADAY PERIODICITY: Heston, Korajczyk & Sadka (Journal of Finance 2010) find stock returns show systematic continuation at half-hour intervals that are exact multiples of a trading day (an effect persisting at least 40 trading days), while at sub-hour horizons short-term REVERSAL dominates, driven by temporary liquidity imbalances and bid-ask bounce. Implication: at 5-minute-to-1-hour horizons in individual stocks, the dominant documented effect is mean reversion plus microstructure noise, not trend - i.e., a short-horizon trend rule trades against the grain of measured intraday autocorrelation.
- TIME-OF-DAY: volume, volatility, spreads and trading activity follow a robust U-shape - highest in the first ~30 minutes, minimum around midday, rising again into the close; bid-ask spreads are widest at the open. Cooper, Cliff & Gulen (2008, 'Return Differences between Trading and Non-Trading Hours: Like Night and Day') show the entire US equity premium 1993-2006 accrued OVERNIGHT; intraday (open-to-close) returns were approximately zero to negative, partly because high opening prices tend to decline over the first hour of trading. The pattern holds for individual stocks, indices, and futures on both NYSE and Nasdaq and has largely persisted since. Lou, Polk & Skouras (JFE 2019, 'A Tug of War') show 14 well-known strategies earn profits either entirely overnight or entirely intraday (momentum profits accrue overnight; value/profitability/investment premia intraday), with overnight-intraday spreads varying on the order of 2%/month.
- VWAP: the academic literature treats VWAP as an EXECUTION benchmark (Berkowitz, Logue & Noser 1988, Journal of Finance; Madhavan 2002 'VWAP Strategies'), used to measure institutional trading cost. There is essentially no peer-reviewed evidence that VWAP crosses/deviations are a standalone alpha signal; claimed VWAP mean-reversion 'edges' come from vendor blogs and unaudited backtests. What is defensible: prices statistically mean-revert toward VWAP intraday partly because execution algos target it, so VWAP is useful for improving entry quality (buy below, not above VWAP), not as a signal engine.
- RETAIL DAY-TRADER BASE RATES ARE DEVASTATING: Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti (2020, 'Day Trading for a Living?') tracked all 19,646 Brazilians who began day trading mini-index futures 2013-2015: 97% of those persisting 300+ sessions lost money; only ~1.1% earned above minimum wage; ~0.4-0.5% earned more than a bank teller (~US$54/day); the single top earner averaged US$310/day with a daily SD of US$2,560; no evidence of learning-by-doing. Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (Taiwan, 1992-2006, 'The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill'): under 1% of day traders predictably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees; the previously top-ranked 500 earn +37.9 bps/day after fees while bottom-ranked earn -28.9 bps/day after fees.
- WHAT LOSES RELIABLY, PER THE LITERATURE: (1) generic MA/oscillator/channel rules on 5-minute bars on liquid US equities - zero value even before costs once data snooping is controlled; (2) intraday long exposure as a class - the average open-to-close return is ~zero/negative while the premium accrues overnight; (3) trading the midday session - lowest volatility and volume, no documented predictability; (4) buying into the open - high opens systematically fade during the first hour; (5) any strategy paying the spread frequently in low-priced stocks - sub-$5 names commonly carry quoted spreads of 0.5-1%+ of price (a single $0.01 tick is 0.5% on a $2 stock; small-cap/low-volume spreads above 1% are routine), so a strategy with 3% stops and multiple round trips per day can hand its entire risk budget to the spread.
- COST ARITHMETIC FOR LOW-PRICED STOCKS: with a minimum one-cent spread, a $2 stock costs >=0.5% per round trip in spread alone (before slippage and commissions); at 2 trades/day that is ~2.5%/week of drag, versus a documented best-case intraday anomaly (last-half-hour momentum) with R-squared of ~1.6% on index ETFs. No published study finds a simple intraday technical rule on individual low-priced equities that survives these costs; the only cost-surviving published intraday results involve index futures/ETFs (low spreads) or high-relative-volume stock selection with breakout logic, from conflicted practitioner sources.

### What it means for this project

- The 5-minute trend bot should be framed (and graded) as an educational cost-measurement exercise, not an alpha attempt: the closest published test (7,846 rules on 5-min SPY bars) found zero value BEFORE costs, and intraday open-to-close returns are on average flat-to-negative. Have the bot log spread paid, slippage vs signal price, and number of round trips, and report 'cost per trade as % of 3% stop distance' - that ratio is the real lesson.
- Sub-$5 universe makes it strictly worse: a one-cent tick is 0.2-0.5%+ of price, quoted spreads of 0.5-1%+ are routine, so each round trip consumes 15-35% of the 3% stop budget before any adverse move. If the educational goal permits, compare the same logic on a liquid ETF (SPY/QQQ) as a control - the performance gap will isolate the microstructure cost of the cheap-stock universe.
- IBKR paper-trading fills plus delayed data will materially overstate 5-minute-bar performance: paper fills ignore queue position and often fill at prices a live order would not get, and 15-minute-delayed quotes make 5-minute signals stale by 3 bars. Treat paper P&L on the intraday bot as an upper bound; log the delay explicitly and consider timestamping signals vs bar close to quantify staleness.
- Capped limit entries are the right instinct on wide-spread names (never cross a 1% spread with a market order), but they create adverse selection on breakout-style entries: fills happen when the move fails and are missed when it works. Log unfilled signals and their counterfactual P&L - published ORB results assume fills that a capped-limit retail order frequently would not get.
- Time-of-day filters are the most literature-supported improvement available: skip new trend entries in the first 30 minutes (widest spreads, high-open fade, U-shape volatility spike), expect nothing from midday (no documented predictability, lowest volume), and if any intraday long bias is coded, the only peer-reviewed slot is the last half-hour conditioned on the sign of the first half-hour (index ETFs, R-squared ~1.6%, weaker post-2013) - an effect too small to overcome sub-$5 spreads but fine to demonstrate on paper.
- Flatten-at-close puts the intraday bot structurally on the wrong side of the overnight/intraday decomposition: since 1993 essentially the whole equity premium accrued overnight, which is exactly the window this bot never holds. The daily-bar SMA bot, which holds overnight, is on the right side of that decomposition - worth stating in the educational writeup as the single biggest structural difference between the two bots.
- If the intraday bot is ever redesigned, the least-bad published template is an opening-range breakout with a relative-volume ('stock in play') filter and defined R-multiple targets, not a continuous MA crossover - but treat the Zarattini/Aziz numbers (Sharpe 2.81, 36% alpha) with heavy skepticism: non-peer-reviewed, authors sell trading education, results lean on leverage and optimistic fill assumptions, and independent replications of simple ORB on index products show the edge decayed. Expect breakeven-at-best after realistic sub-$5 costs.
- Set expectations with base rates in any educational documentation: 97% of persistent Brazilian day traders lost money and <1% of Taiwanese day traders beat fees reliably; there is no published simple intraday technical rule on individual stocks that survives retail transaction costs. A realistic success criterion for the paper system is 'losses fully explained by measured costs and time-of-day effects', not positive P&L.

## Academic evidence on returns of low-priced, high-volatility, lottery-like stocks (Kumar lottery stocks, MAX effect, low-volatility anomaly, penny stocks, reverse splits, sub-$5 dilution)

### Key findings

- Kumar (2009, Journal of Finance): 'lottery-type' stocks are defined as the intersection of below-median price, high idiosyncratic volatility, and high idiosyncratic skewness — essentially the sub-$5/sub-$20 volatile universe. These stocks earn negative risk-adjusted returns, and retail investors who overweight them underperform other investors by roughly 2-3 percentage points per year; underperformance is concentrated among lower-income investors who tilt hardest into them.
- Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw (2011, JFE, 'Maxing Out'): sorting stocks by MAX (average of the 5 highest daily returns in the prior month), the highest-MAX decile underperforms the lowest-MAX decile by more than 1% per MONTH (>12%/yr) in both raw and 4-factor risk-adjusted terms, robust to size, book-to-market, momentum, reversal, liquidity, and skewness controls. Stocks that just printed big daily spikes — exactly what a trend/momentum screen on volatile cheap stocks selects — have the worst forward returns.
- Ang, Hodrick, Xing & Zhang (2006, JF): the idiosyncratic-volatility puzzle — the spread between the lowest and highest idiosyncratic-volatility quintiles exceeds 1% per month, with high-IVOL stocks earning abnormally LOW returns. The effect replicates internationally (AHXZ 2009) and is strongest among small, low-priced, illiquid stocks. Follow-on work (e.g., Gorman & Welch) argues part of it is short-term overreaction/reversal rather than pure lottery preference — either way, forward returns after volatility spikes are poor.
- Low-volatility anomaly: Blitz & van Vliet (2007, JPM) find a ~12% annualized alpha spread between global low- and high-volatility decile portfolios (1986-2006), present separately in the US, Europe, and Japan. Baker, Bradley & Wurgler (2011, FAJ) show that over 1968-2008 high-volatility and high-beta US stocks 'substantially underperformed'; in their famous illustration a dollar in the lowest-volatility quintile compounded to roughly $59 while a dollar in the highest-volatility quintile fell to well under $1 in real terms. They attribute persistence to leverage constraints and benchmark-relative institutional mandates that stop arbitrageurs from shorting the junk.
- Important nuance: the high-vol underperformance is concentrated in the extreme deciles — small, cheap, unprofitable, hard-to-short stocks (the exact sub-$5 cohort). The anomaly is mostly 'the worst high-vol stocks are terrible,' not 'low-vol stocks are magic.' Because the bad leg is expensive to short, long-only retail traders cannot harvest the anomaly; they can only avoid being its counterparty.
- Penny/OTC stocks: Eraker & Ready (2015, JFE) study ~6,800 OTC stocks (2000-2008): returns are negative on average and highly positively skewed (a few huge winners, most go toward zero); an OTC portfolio underperforms listed stocks by roughly 2% per month, and sampled investors averaged about -24% annualized after transaction costs. SEC DERA white paper (White, 2016, 'Outcomes of Investing in OTC Stocks'): 1.8 million trades by 200,000+ retail investors — median holding-period return -11.9% (mean -8.3%, both significant at 1%), worse for promoted stocks and weaker-disclosure tiers; it also cites prior academic estimates of -27% mean / -37% median annual OTC returns for 2001-2010. Caveat: these numbers are for OTC/pink-sheet stocks; exchange-listed sub-$5 names are less catastrophic but still sit in the worst MAX/IVOL deciles.
- Reverse splits are a bearish signal, not a reset: Desai & Jain (1997) find -10.8% one-year and -33.9% three-year abnormal returns after reverse splits; Kim, Klein & Rosenfeld (2008, Financial Management; 1,612 reverse splits 1962-2001) confirm significantly negative 3-year abnormal returns plus deteriorating operating performance, and note the stocks are nearly impossible to short (which is why the mispricing persists). Survivability studies find most reverse-splitting firms are not viable long-run and many are eventually delisted. Reverse-split volume among listed stocks hit ~495 in 2023 (vs 288 in 2022), and Nasdaq has tightened rules against serial sub-$1 reverse-splitters.
- Dilution machinery of sub-$5 companies: cash-burning microcaps finance via ATMs, PIPEs, registered directs, warrants, and floating-price converts, typically selling INTO price strength (spikes attract supply, truncating trends) and announcing offerings overnight (gap-down risk). Hillion & Vermaelen (2004, JFE) find firms issuing 'death-spiral' (floating-price) convertibles earn about -1.8% per month (~-22%/yr) afterward. The classic new-issues puzzle (Loughran & Ritter 1995) shows equity issuers underperform non-issuers by roughly 5 pp/yr over five years. SEO announcements themselves carry significantly negative abnormal returns (single-digit negative CARs, worse in stressed periods).
- Behavioral mechanism: Birru & Wang (2016, JFE, 'Nominal Price Illusion') show investors systematically overestimate the upside skewness ('room to grow') of low-priced stocks; skewness expectations jump when a stock splits to a lower price even though actual skewness falls. This demand pressure is why the lottery cohort stays chronically overpriced. Han & Kumar (2013, JFQA) show stocks dominated by retail trading with lottery features carry significantly negative alphas.
- Bottom line on the direction and size of the effect: high-volatility, low-priced stocks systematically UNDERPERFORM as a class. Best point estimates: >1%/month (>12%/yr) risk-adjusted shortfall for the top MAX/IVOL decile vs the bottom; ~12%/yr low-vol decile alpha spread globally; -8% to -37%/yr average realized returns in the OTC segment; -10% to -34% abnormal returns over 1-3 years post-reverse-split. Returns are extremely right-skewed: the mean is propped up by rare multi-baggers while the median stock loses, so median outcomes are far worse than the (already negative) mean.

### What it means for this project

- Both bots trade inside the historically worst-performing corner of the US equity market. A long-only system on volatile sub-$20 and sub-$5 stocks fights a structural headwind on the order of 1%/month risk-adjusted vs the market (MAX/IVOL decile evidence). Expected value of buy-and-hold exposure to this universe is negative even before costs — any edge must come entirely from timing, and the timing signal must overcome ~12%/yr of adverse drift.
- The SMA-crossover entry has an adverse-selection problem baked in: a crossover on a volatile cheap stock usually triggers right after large up-days, i.e., it systematically buys high-MAX-month stocks — the cohort with the worst documented forward returns (>1%/month underperformance) and strong short-term reversal. Consider filters that avoid entering immediately after outlier daily spikes (e.g., skip entries when the prior 5-day gain or the max daily return in the last month is in the top decile of the universe).
- Expect P&L to be lottery-shaped: most trades small losers, results dominated by a few outliers. Evaluate the paper results on median trade P&L, hit rate, and drawdown — not mean return — and run enough trades (hundreds) before believing anything; a single 3x winner can mask a negative-edge system for months.
- The 3% stop on 5-minute bars sits well inside normal noise for sub-$5 high-volume names (daily ranges of 5-15% are routine, and percentage spreads are large — a 2-cent spread on a $2 stock is 1% round-trip). Anticipate very frequent stop-outs where cumulative spread + stop losses quietly bleed the account; log stop-out frequency explicitly, since paper fills at delayed prices will understate this bleed vs live trading.
- Flatten-at-close on the intraday bot is genuinely protective and consistent with the literature: offerings, dilutive financings, reverse splits, and halts in this universe are announced overnight and cause double-digit gap-downs. Conversely, the DAILY bot holds overnight and bears full dilution/gap risk — consider universe filters: exclude stocks with an active S-3 shelf/ATM, rapid share-count growth, going-concern language, or a reverse split in the past 1-2 years (post-reverse-split names show -10% to -34% abnormal returns over 1-3 years).
- Price spikes in sub-$5 names attract supply from the companies themselves (ATMs/PIPEs sell into strength), which truncates exactly the trends the bots are trying to ride. Expect trend persistence to be weaker in this universe than in mid/large caps; a trend system validated on liquid large caps will not transfer.
- Delayed data + capped limit entries create optimistic fills in paper trading: in fast movers, resting limits fill disproportionately when price is trading through them (you get filled when you're about to be wrong), and IBKR paper fills overstate available liquidity in thin names. Treat paper results as an upper bound; haircut them by realistic slippage (at least the full spread per side on sub-$5 names).
- Stay strictly on exchange-listed stocks and never extend the universe to OTC/pink sheets: the measured retail outcome there is -8% to -37% per year with heavy manipulation/promotion activity — an order of magnitude worse than listed sub-$5 names.
- For the educational write-up, the honest framing is: these bots are an experiment in whether a mechanical trend rule can extract positive timing alpha from a universe with documented negative average returns, overpricing driven by retail lottery demand (nominal price illusion), and issuer dilution — the deck is stacked, which is precisely what makes the measurement interesting on paper money rather than real money.

## Trend-following / time-series momentum robustness: where it works, parameter sensitivity, post-publication decay, and the (lack of) evidence for MA rules on individual volatile stocks

### Key findings

- ORIGINAL EVIDENCE IS MULTI-ASSET FUTURES, NOT SINGLE STOCKS. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (JFE 2012) documented time-series momentum (TSMOM) in 58 liquid futures/forwards (equity indices, currencies, commodities, sovereign bonds), 1965-2009: past 12-month return positively predicts the next ~1 month, persisting about a year then partially reversing. All 58 contracts showed positive TSMOM returns (52 statistically significant), and the DIVERSIFIED 58-market portfolio had a Sharpe of ~1.0 gross - but individual markets each had much lower Sharpe; the portfolio result comes from combining many imperfectly correlated trend streams.
- CENTURY-SCALE CONFIRMATION, SAME CAVEAT. Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen 'A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing' (1880-2016, 67 markets) found positive returns in every decade and gains in 8 of the 10 worst 60/40 drawdowns, with per-market Sharpe averaging only ~0.4 - the strategy is a low-Sharpe-per-market, high-breadth strategy. Levine & Pedersen 'Which Trend Is Your Friend?' (FAJ 2016) showed TSMOM and moving-average crossovers are mathematically equivalent representations (both are linear filters, spanning 30-360 day lookbacks); many rule variants give similar results, so implementation quality (costs, diversification, position sizing, risk management) matters more than which crossover you pick.
- PARAMETER ROBUSTNESS: the Sharpe surface across 1-10 month (roughly 20-250 trading day) lookbacks is nearly flat with no clear optimum; 3-12 month signals remained economically and statistically significant post-2008. But robustness breaks down for FAST signals: sub-monthly lookbacks depend on positive short-horizon autocorrelation that individual stocks do not reliably have, and short-lookback profitability is heavily eroded by whipsaw and microstructure costs (bid-ask bounce accounts for much of apparent short-horizon alpha).
- STATISTICAL FRAGILITY EVEN WHERE IT 'WORKS'. Huang, Li, Wang & Zhou 'Time series momentum: Is it there?' (JFE 2020) re-ran MOP asset-by-asset: 47 of 55 assets had t-stats below 1.65; the pooled regression overstates predictability, and the TSMOM strategy's performance was statistically indistinguishable from a static strategy based on each asset's historical mean return (i.e., much of TSMOM 'alpha' is long-bias plus volatility scaling, not trend timing).
- POST-PUBLICATION DECAY IS THE NORM. McLean & Pontiff (JoF 2016), studying 97 published cross-sectional predictors: returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication. Practitioner reviews (Alpha Architect) characterize trend/TSMOM excess returns as 'substantial, fading, non-persistent and highly methodology-sensitive.' A standard practitioner heuristic is to haircut backtested Sharpe by roughly half.
- POST-2015 OUT-OF-SAMPLE RECORD IS LUMPY, NOT DEAD. The SG Trend Index (10 largest trend CTAs) had a near-lost decade 2009-2018: under ~2% annualized with a ~22% max drawdown while the S&P 500 compounded ~13%/yr. Then 2022 delivered ~+27% ('crisis alpha' year when stocks and bonds both fell double digits), 2024 was ~+2.4%, and YTD 2026 is ~+7% (as of Aug 2026). AQR's 'You Can't Always Trend When You Want' (JPM 2020) attributes the weak 2010s to unusually small risk-adjusted market moves, not crowding or a broken trend-to-profit mechanism - i.e., long multi-year flat stretches are an expected feature.
- MOVING-AVERAGE RULES ON STOCKS/INDICES: CLASSIC RESULTS DID NOT SURVIVE. Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (JoF 1992) found MA rules beat benchmarks on the DJIA 1897-1986, but out-of-sample profits were non-existent in the period after the paper (LeBaron 2000; Fang et al. 2013; Taylor 2014), with data snooping the leading explanation. Zakamulin's out-of-sample tests with realistic transaction costs (Journal of Asset Management 2014 and later book/papers) found no statistically significant outperformance of MA/TSMOM timing rules in the later half of the sample, many false signals in both bull and bear markets, and benefit concentrated almost entirely in bear-market downside protection.
- SINGLE-STOCK TIME-SERIES MOMENTUM EXISTS ONLY IN DIVERSIFIED, LONG-SHORT, MONTHLY FORM. D'Souza, Srichanachaichok, Wang & Yao (1927-2014 US, 1975+ international) found single-stock TSMOM profitable (dual TS+cross-sectional momentum ~1.88%/month), robust across sub-periods and sizes - but this is a portfolio of hundreds of stocks, monthly formation/holding, long-short, before retail-level costs. No academic support exists for daily or intraday MA crossovers on a handful of individual volatile names.
- LOW-PRICED STOCKS ARE EXPLICITLY EXCLUDED FROM THE EVIDENCE BASE. Academic momentum studies routinely drop stocks under $5 (the SEC's penny-stock cutoff) for illiquidity and microstructure reasons. Sub-$5, high-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks are 'lottery stocks' (Kumar 2009; Bali-Cakici-Whitelaw MAX effect) with documented NEGATIVE average abnormal returns because retail demand overprices them - quant funds exclude them to IMPROVE expected returns. A long-only trend bot on this universe starts with a negative drift headwind the published trend literature never faced.
- INTRADAY HORIZONS ARE REVERSAL-DOMINATED FOR INDIVIDUAL STOCKS. High-frequency studies find average intraday return autocorrelations for US stocks are mostly NEGATIVE, with reversals most pronounced at ~15-minute horizons; intraday momentum in individual stocks is weak and inconsistent (the robust 'market intraday momentum' result - first half-hour predicting last half-hour - is an index-level effect). There is also a documented end-of-day reversal effect (Baltussen, Da & Soebhag), and much of small-stock drift accrues overnight, which a flatten-at-close bot forfeits by construction.

### What it means for this project

- Treat the two bots as tests of DIFFERENT hypotheses than the published trend literature supports. The research validates slow (3-12 month) trend signals diversified across many uncorrelated markets; it does not validate daily SMA crossovers on a few volatile sub-$20 stocks, and it actively argues against 5-minute trend rules on sub-$5 names (negative intraday autocorrelation, reversal at 5-15 min horizons). Frame expected outcome honestly: the daily bot is a weak-evidence experiment; the intraday bot is trading against the documented sign of the effect.
- Do not optimize the SMA pair. The Sharpe surface across lookbacks is nearly flat, so any backtest difference between e.g. 10/50 and 20/100 on a handful of tickers is almost certainly noise; picking the best-performing pair is pure data snooping (the exact failure mode of the post-BLL literature). Prefer one slow, pre-registered parameter set - or an average of 2-3 speeds - and never change it based on recent P&L.
- Get breadth, or accept noise. The engine of every positive trend result is diversification across many imperfectly correlated instruments. With $20k, the closest evidence-backed analog is a slow trend/absolute-momentum sleeve across 5-10 liquid, uncorrelated ETFs (equity index, bonds, gold, commodities, dollar) as a benchmark sleeve; the sub-$20 single-stock bot should be evaluated against it to teach why breadth matters.
- Budget for long whipsaw droughts and judge over large samples. Institutional trend went ~10 years (2009-2018) at under 2% annualized with a ~22% drawdown before +27% in 2022. A daily-bar bot on 3-5 tickers generates so few independent signals that a year of results is statistically meaningless; log every trade and evaluate only after hundreds of round trips, expecting a low win rate with occasional large winners (right-skew) - if the paper bot shows a smooth equity curve, suspect the simulation, not celebrate the edge.
- Haircut every backtest ~50% and stress transaction costs. Apply the McLean-Pontiff/practitioner haircut to any backtested Sharpe, and re-run backtests with pessimistic frictions: on sub-$5 stocks assume 0.5-1%+ effective spread per round trip plus slippage on stops. IBKR paper fills at delayed prices on thin low-priced names will materially overstate fills on both capped limit entries (you'll be 'filled' on entries a real order would miss - adverse selection means paper fills you get are the ones you least want) and 3% stops (real stop slippage through gaps is unmodeled). Track a paper-vs-theoretical fill discrepancy metric explicitly.
- The sub-$5 universe carries a structural negative-drift tax. Lottery-stock evidence implies the long side of high-volatility, low-priced names underperforms on average; academic momentum excludes exactly this universe. For the educational system, this is a feature to demonstrate: run the same 5-min logic on a liquid mid/large-cap control group and show the gap. Also verify the bot's 3% stop is wider than the typical 5-min bar range of these names, or the stop itself becomes the dominant (loss-realizing) exit.
- Flatten-at-close has a hidden cost worth measuring: much of small-stock return accrues overnight, and there is a documented end-of-day reversal pattern; an intraday-only long trend bot systematically forfeits overnight drift while concentrating exposure in the reversal-prone afternoon. Log entry/exit times and decompose P&L by time-of-day to make this visible.
- Expected teaching outcome, stated up front: the most probable honest result is that the daily diversified-style logic roughly breaks even or shows a small edge that is statistically indistinguishable from zero at this sample size, while the 5-min sub-$5 bot loses steadily to spread, whipsaw, and adverse fills - which is itself the research-consistent result. Any strongly positive paper result should first be audited for delayed-data/fill artifacts before being believed.

## Position sizing and risk management evidence: volatility targeting, ATR sizing, fractional Kelly, stop-loss research, and drawdown-based deleveraging

### Key findings

- VOLATILITY TARGETING - the headline result: Moreira & Muir (Journal of Finance 2017) show that scaling exposure by the inverse of recent realized variance produced positive alphas and materially higher Sharpe ratios for the market, momentum, profitability, ROE and investment factors, plus large utility gains for mean-variance investors. Mechanism: volatility spikes are not compensated by proportionally higher expected returns, so cutting size when vol is high wins.
- VOLATILITY TARGETING - the replication caveat: Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (Journal of Financial Economics 2020) tested 103 equity strategies and found volatility-managed portfolios do NOT systematically beat unmanaged ones in direct comparisons; real-time implementable (out-of-sample) versions generally earn LOWER certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios than the unmanaged originals, due to structural instability in the spanning regressions. The in-sample alphas largely come from combining managed+unmanaged, not from vol management alone.
- VOLATILITY TARGETING - where it robustly works: Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison & Van Hemert (Journal of Portfolio Management 2018; Man Group), 60+ assets, data back to 1926: vol targeting raises Sharpe only for 'risk assets' (equities, credit) that exhibit the leverage effect; effect on bonds/FX/commodities is negligible. But across ALL asset classes it reliably cuts the probability and severity of left-tail returns and reduces max drawdown, because crashes cluster in high-vol periods when the targeted portfolio is small. Consensus: the robust, replicable benefit of vol scaling is tail/drawdown reduction, not higher average return.
- VOLATILITY TARGETING on momentum specifically: Barroso & Santa-Clara (JFE 2015) scale a momentum portfolio by inverse 6-month realized vol: Sharpe 0.53 -> 0.97 (nearly doubled), excess kurtosis 18.24 -> 2.68, skewness -2.47 -> -0.42; crashes virtually eliminated. Trend/momentum strategies are the best-documented beneficiaries of inverse-vol sizing.
- CONDITIONAL vol targeting beats continuous: Bongaerts, Kang & van Dijk (Financial Analysts Journal 2020) find conventional continuous vol targeting fails to consistently help in global equity markets and can WORSEN drawdowns; adjusting exposure only in extreme high/low-vol states consistently improves Sharpe, cuts drawdowns and tail risk, with far lower turnover.
- ATR-BASED SIZING: essentially no peer-reviewed academic literature exists on ATR sizing per se; it is the per-trade implementation of inverse-vol sizing (shares = risk dollars / (ATR-multiple stop distance)). Practitioner backtests (QuantifiedStrategies, quant blogs) show its benefit as equalized dollar risk per trade, steadier 'portfolio heat', and better return/max-drawdown ratio versus fixed-dollar or fixed-percent-of-account sizing - consistent with the academic inverse-vol tail-reduction result. It normalizes risk; it does not manufacture return.
- FRACTIONAL KELLY: MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba ('good and bad properties of the Kelly criterion') establish that full Kelly maximizes asymptotic growth but with violent paths - in continuous approximation the probability of EVER falling to fraction x of starting wealth is ~x under full Kelly (50% chance of halving at some point); simulations of ~1000 bets show routine 50-80% drawdowns. Betting c x Kelly delivers approximately (2c - c^2) of maximum growth: half-Kelly captures ~75% of growth with roughly half the volatility and drastically lower drawdown probability (~x^3 for halving, i.e. ~12.5%).
- FRACTIONAL KELLY - estimation error is the killer: Kelly fractions are hypersensitive to edge estimates. A win-rate estimated from ~100 trades has a standard error of ~+/-5 percentage points; overestimating a 55% win rate as 60% makes full Kelly an over-bet, and betting ABOVE true Kelly both cuts growth and raises risk (asymmetric penalty). This is why Thorp and practitioners use half-Kelly or less, and why for a new strategy with a short track record, Kelly math effectively collapses to 'risk a small fixed fraction'.
- STOP-LOSSES - regime-dependence is the core result: Kaminski & Lo ('When Do Stop-Loss Rules Stop Losses?', Journal of Financial Markets 2014) prove the 'stopping premium' is positive when returns have positive serial correlation (momentum) and proportional to that persistence; under a pure random walk simple stop-loss rules ALWAYS lower expected return; under mean reversion (AR(1)) stops actively hurt (you exit right before the bounce). Empirically their rule added ~50-100 bps per month versus buy-and-hold during stop-out periods (1950-2004 data).
- STOP-LOSSES on momentum stocks: Han, Zhou & Zhu ('Taming Momentum Crashes', 1926-2013 data) apply a 10% stop to monthly momentum decile portfolios: worst monthly loss improves from -49.79% to -11.36% (equal-weighted) and -64.97% to -23.28% (value-weighted); average monthly return rises 1.01% -> 1.73%; monthly std falls 6.07% -> 4.67%; Sharpe more than doubles. Strong evidence that stops help TREND/momentum systems.
- STOP-LOSSES - the fine print: broader studies (Massey University essays on stop-loss rules; Lund University stop-loss vs buy-and-hold) find NO consistent outperformance from stops overall - performance correlates with the asset's return autocorrelation. And fixed-percentage stops do not actually cap losses: overnight gaps blow through stop prices and volatile low-priced stocks suffer material slippage, so realized loss can be a multiple of the nominal stop distance. Stops' one unconditional benefit is behavioral (countering the disposition effect) - which a bot doesn't need.
- DRAWDOWN-BASED DELEVERAGING: little rigorous academic literature on explicit equity-curve ratchets. What is well-supported: (1) fixed-fractional sizing (risking a percent of CURRENT equity) automatically deleverages in drawdowns and keeps ruin probability roughly constant; practitioner risk-of-ruin math shows doubling risk per trade roughly squares ruin probability, and typical Monte Carlo results give 95th-percentile worst drawdowns of roughly 18% at 1% risk/trade, roughly 32% at 2%, and 60%+ at 5%; (2) index-level 'risk control' overlays combining vol targeting with drawdown caps show lower vol, higher Sharpe, much lower max drawdown (CSSA risk-control index work); (3) trend-following overlays are the best-documented drawdown mitigator at portfolio level (Alpha Architect 'Avoiding the Big Drawdown').
- CROSS-POSITION WEIGHTING: naive risk parity / inverse-volatility weighting across concurrent positions beats equal-dollar weighting on Sharpe when positions have similar expected Sharpe ratios and correlations (ReSolve risk-parity whitepaper; QuantPedia case studies) - it is the theoretically optimal weighting under the equal-Sharpe assumption. For a handful of similar small-cap positions this is a low-cost improvement over equal dollar amounts.
- SYNTHESIS - what actually has evidence for a small multi-position account: (1) size positions off volatility so each trade risks a constant small fraction of equity (robust, multiple independent literatures); (2) keep per-trade risk in the 0.5-2% range - ruin math, Kelly estimation error, and drawdown percentiles all converge on this; (3) use stops in trend systems, skip or loosen them in mean-reversion systems; (4) expect vol management to cut tails and drawdowns, not to raise average returns (the return-enhancement claims largely failed out-of-sample replication).

### What it means for this project

- Size every entry by risk, not by dollars of stock: shares = (equity x risk%) / stop distance, with risk% at 0.5-1% of the $20k (= $100-200 per trade). On sub-$20 volatile names this is the single most evidence-backed rule available - it is the per-trade version of the inverse-vol scaling that survives academic scrutiny (tail reduction), and it automatically deleverages in drawdowns because risk is a percent of current equity.
- Make the stop distance volatility-scaled (ATR-based), then derive size from it - not the reverse. For the daily SMA bot: stop = 2-3x ATR(14); shares = $150 / (2.5 x ATR). A fixed 3% stop on a stock with 8% daily ATR sits inside one bar of noise; Kaminski-Lo shows noise-triggered stops on weakly-autocorrelated returns are pure expected-return drag.
- The 5-minute bot's flat 3% stop is the weakest link: sub-$5 high-volume stocks routinely move 3% in minutes, and 5-minute bar returns are close to a random walk - the regime where Kaminski-Lo prove stops REDUCE expected return. Either widen to an ATR multiple (e.g., 1.5-2x ATR(14) of 5-min bars) so the stop sits outside normal noise, or accept that this stop is a catastrophe cap, not an edge, and size accordingly. Flatten-at-close is genuinely valuable though: it eliminates overnight gap risk, which is the main way percentage stops fail to cap losses.
- The daily trend bot holds overnight, so its 'max loss per trade' is NOT the stop distance - gaps on volatile sub-$20 stocks can double or triple it. Budget worst-case loss at 2-3x the nominal stop when setting risk% (i.e., if you want a true 1% worst case, size for ~0.4-0.5% nominal risk), and verify this against realized gap statistics in the paper-trade logs.
- Stops are on the right side of the evidence for BOTH bots only because both are trend-followers (positive serial correlation is the one regime where stops add expected return - Kaminski-Lo, Han-Zhou-Zhu). If a mean-reversion bot is ever added, do not port the stop logic over; the same rule flips from helpful to harmful.
- Cap total portfolio heat: sum of open risk across all positions <= 4-6% of equity (e.g., max 5-6 concurrent positions at ~1% each). With correlated low-priced small-caps (they crash together), also cap gross exposure - e.g., never more than ~100% of the $20k deployed, scaled down toward 50% when portfolio-level realized vol spikes into its top decile (the 'conditional' version has better evidence and far less churn than continuous daily rescaling).
- Do NOT compute a Kelly fraction from paper-trading results and use it. With fewer than several hundred trades, the win-rate standard error (~+/-5pp at 100 trades) makes the Kelly estimate noise, and over-betting is asymmetrically punished. Fixed-fractional 0.5-1% per trade approximates quarter-to-half Kelly for any realistic retail edge; revisit only after 300+ logged trades, and even then use at most half the computed fraction.
- Add one simple drawdown ratchet on top of fixed-fractional sizing: halve risk% per trade when account equity is >10% below its high-water mark, restore at a new high. Evidence here is practitioner-grade, not academic, but it is cheap, mechanical, and aligned with the risk-control-index results; avoid anything fancier (continuous equity-curve feedback tends to be curve-fit).
- When both bots hold multiple concurrent positions, allocate risk budget inverse to each name's ATR%-of-price (naive risk parity) rather than equal dollars - modest but consistently positive Sharpe improvement when positions have similar expected edge.
- Set expectations correctly for the educational writeup: the honest lesson from this literature is that volatility-aware sizing and stops in trend systems primarily buy SURVIVAL (smaller tails, shallower drawdowns, near-zero ruin probability at 1% risk) rather than higher returns - the return-boost claims for vol management largely failed out-of-sample (Cederburg et al.). For a $20k account, survival is the whole game: a 33% return recovers a 25% drawdown, but a 50% drawdown needs +100%.
- Delayed data + capped limit entries interact with sizing: stop triggers will fire late and fills on sub-$5 names carry 0.2-1% spread cost per side. Add a slippage haircut (e.g., assume stop fills 0.5-1% worse than trigger on sub-$5 names) in both the sizing math and the paper-trade performance accounting, or the backtest will overstate how well the 3% stop caps losses.

## Retail execution quality: effective spreads in low-priced stocks, limit vs market orders, IBKR order handling (Adaptive/Midprice/SMART), PFOF effects, and live-vs-backtest slippage gaps

### Key findings

- TICK-SIZE FLOOR DOMINATES LOW-PRICED STOCK SPREADS: Reg NMS Rule 612 bans sub-penny quotes at/above $1.00, so a $2.50 stock has a minimum quoted spread of $0.01 = 40 bps and a $5 stock 20 bps; many high-volume sub-$5 Nasdaq names are permanently 'tick-constrained' (pinned at 1 cent wide). Below $1.00, quoting in $0.0001 increments is legal, so percentage spreads can compress but displayed depth thins. The SEC's 2024 Reg NMS amendment introducing half-penny ticks for tick-constrained stocks (time-weighted avg quoted spread <= $0.015) was upheld by the D.C. Circuit (Oct 2025) but implementation was pushed by exemptive relief to the first business day of November 2026 — until then the 1-cent floor stands.
- MEASURED RETAIL EFFECTIVE SPREADS (Dyhrberg, Shkilko & Werner, 'The Retail Execution Quality Landscape', Journal of Financial Economics 2025, Rule 605 data): wholesaler effective spreads average ~49 bps vs quoted NBBO of ~53-70 bps at execution time; wholesalers price-improve 65.7% of retail orders vs only 9.5% on exchanges; wholesaler realized spreads are ~0.76x quoted spread vs 0.97x on exchanges; retail traders in SMALL stocks benefit most from wholesaler internalization. A Wharton PFOF study finds wholesalers return ~24 cents of each $1 of spread revenue to retail as price improvement (~1 cent goes to brokers as PFOF, ~75 cents retained).
- LIMIT ORDERS MEASURABLY BEAT MARKETABLE ORDERS FOR RETAIL: Anand, Samadi, Sokobin & Venkataraman ('Retail Limit Orders', Review of Finance 2025; 27M+ retail orders, 19 brokers, FINRA OATS, May 2020) find limit orders reduce retail trading costs by ~10 bps on average vs marketable orders, with the advantage LARGEST exactly where this project trades: wide spreads, high volatility, small-cap stocks. ~25% of retail orders are limits; 74% are placed behind the NBBO. The offsetting cost is non-execution risk and adverse fill selection — passive limits fill disproportionately when price is moving against you, which matters for trend-following entries where the missed fills are systematically the winners.
- BROKER CHOICE AND ORDER TYPE SWAMP PFOF: Schwarz, Barber, Huang, Jorion & Odean ('The Actual Retail Price of Equity Trades', Journal of Finance 2025; 85,000 simultaneous real-money market orders, ~$15M, 128 stocks, Dec 2021-Jun 2022) found round-trip execution costs ranged 0.07% (TD Ameritrade) to 0.46% across six accounts at five brokers — and IBKR was at the BOTTOM for small market orders: IBKR Pro ~0.44% round trip with price improvement of only ~19% of the half-spread (vs 47% at TD), IBKR Lite ~0.46%. Variation in PFOF did NOT explain execution differences; wholesalers simply give different prices to different brokers' flow (likely adverse-selection pricing of IBKR's more sophisticated customer base, and IBKR Pro's exchange-directed SMART routing forgoes wholesaler sub-penny price improvement on tiny orders). Implication: on IBKR specifically, naked market orders are the most expensive way to trade small size.
- IBKR ORDER TYPES THAT ADDRESS THIS: (a) Midprice order — pegs to the NBBO midpoint (US stocks, SMART-routed), capturing ~half the quoted spread when it fills, at the cost of fill uncertainty; on a tick-constrained $3 stock that is ~16 bps saved per side vs crossing the spread. (b) Adaptive algo (market or limit variant) — works the order between bid and ask with Normal/Urgent/Patient priority; IBKR claims better average fills than regular market/limit orders, but this is self-reported marketing (as are IBKR's TAG/third-party 'best price execution' statistics) with no independent academic verification. (c) SMART routing continuously re-routes among exchanges plus ~8 dark pools; IBKR Pro does not accept PFOF for stock orders while IBKR Lite flow is sold to wholesalers — yet Schwarz et al. measured Pro and Lite nearly identically for small market orders, so 'no PFOF' should not be assumed to mean 'better fills' at retail size.
- PFOF POLICY CONTEXT: the SEC's 2022 Order Competition Rule proposal estimated retail marketable orders routed to wholesalers (~90% of such flow) suffer a 'competitive shortfall' of ~1.08 bps per dollar traded (~$1.5B/year aggregate) vs order-by-order auctions; the proposal was withdrawn in 2025. Net read of the literature: wholesaler execution is genuinely better than exchange execution for small retail orders, PFOF's direct cost is small relative to spread costs, and cross-broker execution dispersion (up to ~39 bps round trip on identical trades) is the far bigger lever.
- LIVE-VS-BACKTEST GAP IS MOSTLY OVERFITTING PLUS UNMODELED FRICTIONS: Wiecki, Campbell, Lent & Stauth ('All That Glitters Is Not Gold', 2016; 888 real Quantopian strategies with 6+ months out-of-sample) found backtest Sharpe predicts out-of-sample performance with R^2 < 0.025 (essentially nothing), and the more a quant backtested, the bigger the in-sample/out-of-sample gap. Practitioner claims that realistic friction assumptions cut reported returns 30-50% are blog-grade (unverified vendor content) and should be treated as anecdote, but the direction is consistent: high trade frequency multiplies the friction tax. No credible academic study cleanly isolates 'live minus backtest slippage' for systematic retail traders — this is a genuine evidence gap.
- IBKR PAPER-TRADING FILLS ARE OPTIMISTIC BY CONSTRUCTION: IBKR documents that paper-account fills are simulated from top-of-book only (no depth, no queue position) and that stops and complex orders are always simulated; Elite Trader users report API paper limit orders sometimes filling at prices BETTER than the limit, which essentially never happens live. Paper results therefore overstate fill rates and fill quality, especially for passive limits in thin sub-$5 names.
- INTRADAY TIMING: decades of microstructure evidence (NYSE, Nasdaq, and international markets) shows bid-ask spreads follow a U/reverse-J pattern — widest at the open, narrowing through late morning, ticking up near the close. Entering at or just after the open (typical for a daily-bar bot acting on yesterday's signal) pays the day's widest spreads; the closing auction, by contrast, is the deepest single liquidity event of the day.
- STOP ORDERS CONVERT TO MARKET ORDERS ON TRIGGER: slippage past the stop level is unbounded in fast tapes (flash-crash-type events filled stops far below trigger levels); on volatile sub-$5 stocks a 3% stop on 5-minute bars will trigger frequently and realized losses will routinely exceed 3% by the spread plus any gap. No good academic magnitude estimates exist for retail stop slippage specifically; it must be measured from one's own fills.
- COMMISSION MATH ON LOW-PRICED SHARES (IBKR Pro): per-share pricing is proportionally brutal at low prices. Tiered ($0.0035/share, $0.35 min): a $2,000 clip of a $2.50 stock = 800 shares = $2.80 = ~14 bps per side (~28 bps round trip) before exchange/regulatory fees, i.e., commissions alone rival the effective spread; the same $2,000 in a $15 stock costs ~2.3 bps per side. Fixed pricing ($0.005/share, $1 min) is capped at 1% of trade value, which binds on very low-priced stocks.

### What it means for this project

- Budget realistic per-side costs by price tier, not a flat number: for the 5-min bot in $1-$5 stocks assume half the quoted spread minimum (tick-constrained $2-3 stocks: ~17-25 bps if you get midpoint-ish fills, 33-50 bps if you cross the spread) plus ~14+ bps commission per side at $2k clips; for the daily bot in $5-$20 stocks assume ~5-20 bps spread cost plus 2-8 bps commission. If the backtest edge per trade is under ~40-80 bps round trip in the sub-$5 universe, execution costs alone can erase it.
- Keep the capped limit entries — the evidence (Anand et al. 2025) says limit orders save ~10 bps on average and MORE in exactly this universe (volatile, wide-spread, small-cap) — but log every non-fill and mark the signal's subsequent return, because for trend entries the unfilled orders are disproportionately the winners; measure net-of-missed-trades performance, not just fill-price savings.
- Never send naked market orders on IBKR for entries: Schwarz et al. measured IBKR Pro small market orders at ~0.44% round trip, the worst of the brokers tested. Use marketable limits capped at the NBBO ask (or ask + 1 tick), or IBKR's Midprice order for entries where a few seconds of fill uncertainty is acceptable — on a tick-constrained $3 stock, midpoint execution saves ~16 bps per side vs crossing.
- Treat IBKR's Adaptive algo (Patient/Normal) as a reasonable default for non-urgent entries but recognize its claimed superiority is IBKR marketing, not independent research — A/B test it against plain capped limits in your own fill logs before trusting it.
- For the 5-min bot: skip entries in the first 15-30 minutes (spreads are widest at the open) or add a spread filter that skips the trade when quoted spread exceeds a threshold (e.g., >50 bps). Note that with delayed data you cannot see the live spread — this pushes toward either subscribing to live quotes (cheap for one exchange feed) or hard-capping limit prices off the last delayed bar, accepting more non-fills.
- For flatten-at-close: prefer a Market-on-Close order into the closing auction (deepest liquidity of the day for these names) over a 3:55-3:59 pm marketable order fighting the widening close-time spread.
- Model stop exits pessimistically: a 3% stop on a sub-$5 stock should be backtested as filling at stop price MINUS at least one spread plus a slippage haircut (e.g., 10-50 bps extra, worse on gaps), never at the stop price itself; consider volatility-scaled stops or stop-limits only if the strategy can tolerate occasional non-execution of the stop (usually it cannot).
- Haircut all paper-trading results: IBKR's simulator fills from top-of-book with no queue modeling and API paper limit fills can be better-than-limit. A defensible convention: count a resting limit as filled only if price trades THROUGH the limit by at least one tick (not merely touches it), and add the commission + half-spread model on top; expect live performance below paper even after that (Wiecki et al.: backtest Sharpe has near-zero predictive power, R^2 < 0.025).
- Prefer the $5-$20 leg of the universe for capital: at $2k-$5k clips, per-share commissions and the 1-cent tick make sub-$2.50 stocks structurally expensive (~28+ bps round-trip commission alone at $2.50); if trading sub-$5 names, size orders above ~100 shares so the $0.35 tiered minimum doesn't inflate the effective rate, and consider whether Fixed pricing's 1% cap beats Tiered for very low-priced shares.
- Instrument your own execution-quality loop from day one: record for every order the NBBO at decision time, limit price, fill price, fill/no-fill, and time-to-fill; compute realized effective spread and implementation shortfall monthly. Given the absence of credible published live-vs-backtest slippage numbers for retail systems, your own fill log is the only trustworthy calibration source, and it directly measures whether Adaptive/Midprice/capped-limit choices are earning their keep.
- Watch the November 2026 half-penny tick implementation: it should roughly halve minimum spreads on tick-constrained high-volume low-priced names, mechanically improving this strategy's cost structure — worth re-running cost assumptions when it lands.

# Wave 2 — Machine Learning, Behavior, Institutions, Outcomes & AI

## Synthesis

### Top insights

- The uncomfortable headline number: across every complete (non-self-selected) dataset ever assembled — Taiwan 1992-2006 (Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean 2014), Brazil futures 2013-2015 (Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti 2019), France (AMF 2014), EU CFD accounts (ESMA 2018), US day-trading audits (NASAA 1999) — 70-97% of active retail speculators lose money, and the reliably profitable fraction is 0.5-1%. This base rate has been stable across decades, countries, and technology regimes. Your prior probability of the bot making money is approximately zero; plan accordingly.
- Backtest Sharpe ratios are close to worthless as predictors. Quantopian's own study of 888 real user algorithms with true out-of-sample data (Wiecki et al. 2016) found backtest Sharpe predicted live Sharpe with R-squared < 0.025 — essentially no relationship — and the gap GREW with the number of backtests run. Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado & Zhu (2014) proved the math: after ~1,000 strategy configurations tried, the EXPECTED best backtest Sharpe is ~3.26 on pure noise. A Sharpe of 3 in your backtest is the default output of iteration, not evidence of skill.
- The best ML models in the best academic study explain almost nothing: Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020, RFS) — the benchmark paper, ~30,000 stocks, 60 years, 94 characteristics — got a monthly out-of-sample R-squared of ~0.33-0.40% for the best neural nets. That is the ceiling achieved by top researchers with clean data. And Avramov, Cheng & Metzker (2023) showed that alpha concentrates in microcaps, unrated, and distressed firms — excluding them cut profits 52-77% — i.e., exactly the stocks a retail bot cannot trade at quoted prices.
- Published edges are already dead or dying: McLean & Pontiff (2016) — anomaly returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication. Chen & Velikov (2023) — across 204 anomalies, after effective spreads and post-2005 decay, the average anomaly nets ~4 basis points/month, statistically zero. On $20k that is roughly $10/year, less than any data subscription. Anything you read in a paper, book, or blog post is priced in at retail cost levels.
- Your one genuine, documented edge is behavioral, not predictive: automation removes the disposition effect (Weber & Camerer 1998 — automatic selling largely eliminates it), overtrading (Barber & Odean 2000 — the most active retail quintile underperformed by ~6.5 pp/yr), revenge-trading risk escalation (Coval & Shumway 2005 — even CBOT pros took 16% more risk after morning losses), and the ~1.1 pp/yr timing gap (Morningstar Mind the Gap 2024). This behavioral drag is roughly the size of any realistic retail edge — a bot that merely prevents these mistakes is doing real, measurable work even with a mediocre signal.
- The predicted failure mode of THIS project is you, after the first drawdown: Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015) showed people abandon algorithms right after seeing them err, even when the algorithm is demonstrably better; Goyal & Wahal (2008) showed even professional pension committees fire strategies at the bottom. The countermeasure is pre-committed, written kill/override rules and (per Dietvorst 2018) one small bounded knob instead of veto power.
- LLM sentiment strategies are contaminated and crowded: Sarkar & Vafa showed Llama 2 'predicted' Covid from 2019 earnings calls in >25% of cases (it remembered the future from training data), and prompting it to pretend it's 2019 does not fix this. Lopez-Lira & Tang's famous 3.8 Sharpe was pre-cost, concentrated in illiquid small caps, partly in a non-tradable overnight reaction, and decayed after ChatGPT's public release. Never backtest an LLM on pre-cutoff dates; treat 'GPT reads headlines' as a crowded, educational-only pipeline exercise.
- Institutional edges are structurally closed to you, not just hard: HFT races last 5-10 microseconds and 6 firms win 80%+ of them (Aquilina, Budish & O'Neill 2022) — a broker-API bot is 4-6 orders of magnitude too slow. Medallion's ~66% gross returns (Zuckerman 2019) required ~17x bank-provided basket-option leverage (Senate PSI report 2014), a $10B capacity cap, and a 50.75% win rate across millions of bets — and Renaissance itself could not scale it (RIEF lost ~20% in 2020 while Medallion made +76%). None of this transfers down to $20k.
- Costs, not bad predictions, are the main killer: Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya (2023) found retail options traders lost $2.1B net while paying $6.4B in transaction costs — the costs WERE the loss. Schwarz et al. (2025) found identical retail orders cost 0.07% to 0.46% round-trip depending on broker — broker/execution choice alone moves costs more than most signals' expected edge. Model effective spreads, slippage, and commissions pessimistically from day one, and reject any strategy whose edge is under ~2x modeled costs.

### What the PhDs agree on

- Backtest overfitting is the central disease of quantitative trading. Bailey/Lopez de Prado (Deflated Sharpe Ratio, 2014), Harvey & Liu (haircut Sharpe, 2015), and Quantopian's empirical 888-algorithm study all converge: uncorrected backtest performance is uninterpretable, and the number of trials must be logged and penalized. Lopez de Prado adds that vanilla scikit-learn cross-validation is structurally invalid on overlapping financial labels — use walk-forward or purged K-fold with an embargo.
- Whatever return predictability exists is tiny, concentrated in price-history features, and lives in illiquid corners. Gu-Kelly-Xiu (2020), Jiang-Kelly-Xiu (2023), and Tobek & Hronec (2021) all find momentum (12-1 month), short-term reversal, volatility, and liquidity variables dominate; fundamentals add little at monthly horizons. Avramov et al. (2023), Hou-Xue-Zhang (2020), and Drechsler & Drechsler all find the profits concentrate in microcaps and hard-to-borrow names where retail execution is worst.
- Published effect sizes shrink badly after publication. Even the two sides of the replication-crisis debate — Hou-Xue-Zhang (2020, 65% of 452 anomalies fail t>1.96) versus Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen (2023, most factors replicate) — agree on this: post-publication and out-of-sample returns are substantially weaker, per McLean & Pontiff's (2016) 26%/58% decay numbers.
- Human discretion in execution is a reliable net negative. Grove et al.'s (2000) meta-analysis of 136 studies: mechanical rules beat expert judgment ~10% on average across domains. Odean (1998): the disposition effect costs ~3.4 pp on round trips. Harvey, Rattray, Sinclair & van Hemert (2017): systematic funds match discretionary funds risk-adjusted — automation removes behavioral drag but does not manufacture alpha.
- Transaction costs must be modeled with effective (not quoted) spreads and pessimistic fills, because they routinely exceed the entire edge. Chen & Velikov (2023), Frazzini-Israel-Moskowitz (institutional realized costs are <1/10 of academic estimates — an advantage you don't have), Fischer & Krauss (2018, LSTM profitable pre-cost, ~zero net post-2010), and the retail options evidence all point the same way.
- Skill among retail speculators exists but is extraordinarily rare (~0.5-1%) and there is no evidence of learning-by-doing: Barber-Lee-Liu-Odean (Taiwan) and Chague et al. (Brazil) both find persistence of losses, not improvement — three-quarters of day-trading volume comes from traders with losing histories.
- For LLM-based forecasting, results are only valid with zero training leakage (Ludwig, Mullainathan & Rambachan, NBER 2024/25; Glasserman & Lin 2023; Sarkar & Vafa; Gao, Jiang & Yan 2025). Evaluate only post-cutoff, or anonymize entities; part of published LLM 'alpha' is demonstrably memorization.

### Myths debunked

- MYTH: 'A good backtest means a good strategy.' Quantopian's 888-algorithm dataset: backtest Sharpe predicts out-of-sample Sharpe with R-squared < 0.025. Bailey et al. (2014): a Sharpe of ~3 is the EXPECTED backtest maximum from ~1,000 tries on pure noise.
- MYTH: 'Deep learning / more AI = better returns.' Gu-Kelly-Xiu found shallow nets beat deep ones; the Kelly-Malamud-Zhou vs Nagel 'virtue of complexity' fight (2024-2025) shows even top journals can't agree complexity helps. Real-money AI vehicles lost to the index: AIEQ ~7% vs ~12% annualized for the S&P 500; Eurekahedge AI index 9.8% vs 13.7%; Sentient (evolutionary-AI fund, PhD army) liquidated in under 2 years; Chen & Ren (2022) found AI funds' only edge over human funds was trading less.
- MYTH: 'ChatGPT can pick stocks — look at the 3.8 Sharpe paper.' The Lopez-Lira & Tang number was pre-cost, small-cap-concentrated, partly untradable (overnight reaction), and decayed after ChatGPT's release. Backtests before the model's training cutoff are contaminated by memorization (Sarkar & Vafa's Covid result), and prompting the model to forget doesn't work.
- MYTH: 'You learn to trade by trading; losses are tuition that pays off.' Barber-Lee-Liu-Odean: ~40% of day traders quit within a month, ~7% survive five years, and losers keep trading — 'trading to learn' is compared to playing roulette to learn. Chague et al.: 97% of Brazilians who persisted 300+ days lost money; zero evidence of learning-by-doing.
- MYTH: 'Renaissance proves it's possible, so I can do a small version of it.' Medallion's edge required ~17x institutional basket-option leverage (Senate PSI 2014), microsecond infrastructure, a deliberately enforced ~$10B cap, and a win rate of only ~50.75% across millions of bets. Renaissance's own public funds (RIEF) lost billions while Medallion soared — the edge didn't even transfer within the same firm.
- MYTH: 'Published anomalies/factor strategies are free money.' Hou-Xue-Zhang: 65% of 452 published anomalies fail basic replication once microcaps are de-emphasized. Chen & Velikov: average net expected return ~4 bps/month — statistically zero. McLean & Pontiff: 58% weaker post-publication.
- MYTH: 'Prop-firm challenges are a cheap route to capital.' FPFX data on 300k+ accounts: ~14% ever pass, ~7% of all traders ever get any payout, average payout ~4% of nominal account size (~$4k on a '$100k' account) against ~$800 average fees, and about half of funded traders blow the account within 90 days.
- MYTH: 'Copy the leaderboard winners.' Apesteguia et al. (2020): copy trading causally increases follower risk-taking without improving returns; Pelster & Hofmann (2018): watched eToro leaders hold losers LONGER; leaderboards are extreme survivorship-bias environments (eToro's own disclosure: ~74-76% of its retail CFD accounts lose money).
- MYTH: 'Following momentum/hot stocks the crowd is buying works.' Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz (2022): the top Robinhood-herded stocks earned -4.7% abnormal returns over the following 20 days — intense retail attention is a contrarian signal.
- MYTH: 'Paper-trading results are a fair preview of live results.' Paper fills ignore market impact, queue position, and adverse selection; Buczynski et al. (2021) reviewed 27 peer-reviewed ML trading experiments and found published results systematically fail to translate to live trading. Treat paper P&L as an upper bound.

### Lessons from other people's outcomes

- Quantopian (2011-2020): ~300-500k users, 12M+ backtests, $250M from Steve Cohen, professional vetting — and it still could not extract deployable alpha from crowdsourced retail strategies; the fund underperformed and the platform shut with two weeks' notice. A solo developer should assume their prior is strictly worse. The transferable lesson from their own data: in/out-of-sample gap grows with backtest count; drawdown, volatility, and turnover metrics had some predictive value where Sharpe had none.
- Sentient Investment Management (evolutionary AI, prominent PhD team) liquidated in under two years (Bloomberg 2018); the Rogers AI Global Macro ETF launched 2018 and closed 2019. Full-time AI teams with better data than you routinely fail outright — a hobbyist bot lagging SPY is the normal, documented outcome, not a personal failure.
- Fischer & Krauss (2018): their LSTM strategy was genuinely predictive historically (~0.45%/day pre-cost) and still netted approximately zero after costs post-2010 — a real signal can exist and be worthless to trade because it was arbitraged away. Expect exactly this pattern in anything you replicate.
- The rare documented successes share a profile you don't have yet: Rob Carver (ex-AHL portfolio manager) publishes his real diversified-futures results — roughly +20-24% in a GOOD year, volatility-targeted, full methodology disclosed, built on decades of institutional experience. Kevin Davey's audited +100%/yr Robbins World Cup wins were small-account contest returns at extreme risk, explicitly not sustainable. Sustainable individual systematic trading looks like modest Sharpe (<1) and cost obsession, not triple digits.
- The single best day trader out of ~20,000 in the complete Brazilian dataset earned ~US$310/day with a daily standard deviation of US$2,560 — the absolute right tail of retail day trading is a stressful, volatile, modest income, not wealth.
- Barrot, Kaniel & Sraer (2016) found the one retail-shaped opportunity — patient limit-order liquidity provision in stressed markets earned ~19-40% annualized alpha on paper — but actual retail investors FAILED to capture it because they exited at the wrong times. The edge existed and human behavior destroyed it; a disciplined rules-based bot is, interestingly, the right form factor to even attempt it (per Nagel 2012, expected returns to liquidity provision spike when VIX is high and institutions pull back).
- Institutions fail behaviorally too: Goyal & Wahal (2008) — pension sponsors fire managers at their low point and the replacements do no better. Renaissance's outside investors pulled tens of billions from RIEF at the bottom. The chase-and-capitulate cycle is universal, which is why pre-committed evaluation rules beat judgment at every level of sophistication.

### Implications for this project

- Define success as infrastructure, measurement, and discipline — not P&L. At $20k over months, results are statistically indistinguishable from luck (the best academic models explain ~0.4% of monthly return variance). The honest deliverables are: a correct data/execution pipeline, walk-forward validation with purged CV and an embargo (Lopez de Prado), a trial log feeding a Deflated Sharpe calculation, pessimistic cost modeling, and a research journal.
- Log every configuration you try and deflate accordingly (Bailey-Lopez de Prado 2014; Harvey-Liu 2015). Lock a true holdout period you never touch. If a daily-equity backtest shows Sharpe > ~1.5-2, the likely causes in order are: look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, unrealistic fills, ignored costs, then skill.
- Charge yourself realistic costs on every simulated fill from day one: effective spread (at least the half-spread) + IBKR commissions + slippage, and reject strategies whose edge is under ~2x modeled costs. Restrict the universe to liquid names — the microcap alpha in the literature is not achievable at retail fills (Avramov et al. 2023). Use limit orders in anything illiquid. Avoid options (retail avg 12.6% spreads on weeklies), CFDs/leveraged forex (74-89% regulator-documented loss rates), and the short side (anomaly profits live in hard-to-borrow names — Drechsler & Drechsler).
- Spend feature-engineering budget where the literature finds signal: 12-1 month momentum, 1-month short-term reversal, industry momentum, volatility, and liquidity measures — all computable from free daily OHLCV. Prefer gradient boosting/random forests or shallow nets over deep architectures (GKX), and require the model to beat both an OLS baseline and buy-and-hold SPY out-of-sample, net of costs.
- Automate exits, daily loss limits, and halts INTO THE CODE (Weber & Camerer 1998; Coval & Shumway 2005). Write down, before going live on paper, the exact pre-registered conditions for turning the bot off or modifying it (e.g., drawdown > 1.5x worst backtest drawdown, or end of a 6-month evaluation window) — because Dietvorst et al. predict you will want to abandon it right after its first visible errors, the statistically worst moment.
- Keep a manual-override log: every intervention, record the override P&L vs what the bot would have done. Grove et al. (2000) predicts your overrides net out negative; measuring this on paper is free tuition. Also measure your personal behavior gap: bot's time-weighted return vs your dollar-weighted return including every pause/resize/restart.
- Build nothing latency-sensitive: any edge requiring fills within milliseconds is fictional through a broker API (Aquilina-Budish-O'Neill 2022). Low turnover is your friend — the only robust AI-fund edge Chen & Ren found was trading less. Treat 'the bot correctly chose not to trade' as a success metric.
- If using LLMs: use them only for what they're proven at — parsing/classifying financial text (all BloombergGPT/FinGPT actually demonstrated). Evaluate only on post-training-cutoff dates, record model version and cutoff in logs, anonymize entities for any historical test (Glasserman & Lin), and hand-validate a sample of labels (Ludwig-Mullainathan-Rambachan). Assume headline-sentiment strategies are crowded and decayed.
- Run the identical strategy in backtest and live paper trading simultaneously and measure the divergence — the backtest-to-live gap itself is the core educational payoff and exactly what published papers omit (Buczynski et al. 2021).
- If this ever goes live with real money: size it as tuition — a small fraction of the $20k, liquid low-spread instruments only, position sizes such that total strategy failure costs less than a university course. Broker choice matters measurably (0.07% vs 0.46% round-trip for identical orders — Schwarz et al. 2025). The base rate says the expected monetary value is zero; the expected educational value is the real return. (Note: this synthesis summarizes published research; it is not personalized financial advice.)

## Machine Learning in Trading — The Academic Evidence (Gu-Kelly-Xiu and successors; backtest overfitting and the deflated Sharpe ratio; the factor-zoo replication crisis; documented out-of-sample successes and failures; which features carry signal; and post-publication anomaly decay)

### Key findings

- THE BENCHMARK ML PAPER: Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 'Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning' (Review of Financial Studies, 2020, 33:2223-2273) tested OLS, LASSO, elastic net, PCR/PLS, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, and neural networks on ~30,000 US stocks (1957-2016) with 94 stock characteristics + interactions (~900 signals). Best neural nets achieved a monthly out-of-sample R-squared of only ~0.33-0.40% for individual stocks — tiny in absolute terms but economically meaningful; plain OLS with many predictors had NEGATIVE out-of-sample R-squared. A long-short decile portfolio from the NN4 model earned an annualized Sharpe of 1.35 value-weighted / 2.45 equal-weighted (before costs), vs 0.61/0.83 for a comparable linear model. The gains came almost entirely from shallow nonlinearities and interactions; deeper networks did not help.
- WHAT ML ADDS, AND FOR WHOM: Avramov, Cheng & Metzker, 'Machine Learning vs. Economic Restrictions' (Management Science, 2023, 69:2587-2619) re-ran GKX-style deep-learning signals under real-world constraints: excluding microcaps cut profits by ~64%, excluding firms without credit ratings by ~52%, excluding distressed firms by ~77%. Value-weighted long-short profits largely disappear after reasonable transaction costs because the strategies demand extreme positions and very high turnover. Conclusion: ML alpha is real but concentrates in tiny, illiquid, hard-to-arbitrage stocks — exactly the ones an individual cannot trade at size or at quoted prices.
- BACKTEST OVERFITTING (the math): Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, 'Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism' (Notices of the American Mathematical Society, May 2014) proved that the expected maximum in-sample Sharpe ratio grows with the number of strategy configurations tried even when the TRUE Sharpe is zero — after ~1,000 independent trials the expected best backtest Sharpe is ~3.26 on pure noise (figure repeated in López de Prado's GARP whitepaper 'The 10 Reasons Most Machine Learning Funds Fail'). Companion tools: Bailey & López de Prado's 'Deflated Sharpe Ratio' (Journal of Portfolio Management, 2014) corrects a reported Sharpe for the number of trials, non-normality, and track-record length; their 'Probability of Backtest Overfitting' framework (Journal of Computational Finance) and Harvey & Liu's 'Backtesting' (JPM, 2015) haircut-Sharpe method (nonlinear haircuts; the common flat 50% haircut is called 'a serious mistake') do the same job from a multiple-testing angle.
- WHY ML FUNDS FAIL (practice): López de Prado, 'The 7 Reasons Most Machine Learning Funds Fail' (SSRN 3031282, 2017/2018, expanded in the book 'Advances in Financial Machine Learning', Wiley 2018): (1) the 'Sisyphus paradigm' (armies of siloed PhDs each pressured to find a strategy, guaranteeing false positives), (2) over-differentiated/stationarity-destroying features, (3) inefficient sampling, (4) wrong labeling (he proposes triple-barrier labels), (5) treating non-IID financial samples as IID, (6) cross-validation leakage (fix: purged K-fold with embargo), (7) backtest overfitting. His core claim: standard scikit-learn-style CV is structurally invalid on overlapping financial labels and silently inflates accuracy.
- THE FACTOR ZOO / P-HACKING: Harvey, Liu & Zhu, '...and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns' (Review of Financial Studies, 2016, 29:5-68) catalogued 316 published factors and showed that under multiple-testing corrections a new factor should clear t > 3.0, not the conventional 2.0; Harvey's 2017 AFA Presidential Address argues 'most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false.' Hou, Xue & Zhang, 'Replicating Anomalies' (RFS, 2020, 33:2019-2133) re-tested 452 published anomalies with microcaps de-emphasized (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighting): 65% fail even a t > 1.96 hurdle, 82% fail at t > 2.78, and 96% of trading-frictions anomalies fail. Counterpoint: Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 'Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?' (Journal of Finance, 2023, 78:2465-2518) use a Bayesian hierarchical model and a 93-country dataset to argue most factors DO replicate and cluster into ~13 themes that work out-of-sample — the debate is live, but both sides agree published effect sizes shrink badly after publication and outside megacap-free samples.
- POST-PUBLICATION DECAY: McLean & Pontiff, 'Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?' (Journal of Finance, 2016, 71:5-32) tracked 97 published predictors: returns are 26% lower out-of-sample (data-mining share) and 58% lower post-publication (arbitrage share), with faster decay in liquid, low-cost stocks. Chen & Velikov, 'Zeroing In on the Expected Returns of Anomalies' (Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 2023, 58:968-1004) push further: across 204 anomalies, after accounting for effective bid-ask spreads, post-publication decay, and the post-2005 trading era, the average anomaly's net expected return is ~4 basis points per month — statistically indistinguishable from zero.
- DOCUMENTED OUT-OF-SAMPLE ML RESULTS: Krauss, Do & Huck (European Journal of Operational Research, 2017; deep nets/boosted trees/random forests on S&P 500 statistical arbitrage) and Fischer & Krauss (EJOR, 2018; LSTM, 1992-2015) found ~0.45%/day returns pre-cost in early samples but profitability 'fluctuating around zero after transaction costs' in the post-2010 subperiod — the signal was real historically and was arbitraged away in-sample-period. Real-world corroboration: Sentient Investment Management, a prominent evolutionary-AI hedge fund, liquidated in 2018 after <2 years (Bloomberg, Sept 2018); the Rogers AI Global Macro ETF launched 2018 and liquidated 2019; the Eurekahedge AI Hedge Fund Index averaged ~10.5%/yr 2011-2017 (roughly index-like) then went flat. The canonical success, Renaissance's Medallion fund (Zuckerman, 'The Man Who Solved the Market', 2019: ~66% gross / ~39% net annualized 1988-2018), is deliberately capacity-capped around $10B, employee-only, and built on short-horizon signals with massive infrastructure — while Renaissance's own investor-facing funds (RIEF/RIDA) lost money and shed tens of billions in AUM (RIEF fell while Medallion made +76% in 2020), a documented illustration that top-tier ML alpha does not scale and was never for sale.
- WHICH FEATURES CARRY SIGNAL: Every ML method in Gu-Kelly-Xiu agrees on the same dominant predictor families: (a) price-trend variables — 12-month momentum, industry momentum, 1-month short-term reversal; (b) liquidity variables — market cap, dollar volume, bid-ask spread; (c) risk variables — return volatility, idiosyncratic volatility, beta. Fundamental/accounting ratios add comparatively little at monthly horizons. Jiang, Kelly & Xiu, '(Re-)Imag(in)ing Price Trends' (Journal of Finance, 2023, 78:3193-3249) trained CNNs directly on price-volume chart images; learned patterns beat hand-coded momentum/reversal rules, transfer across horizons and to international markets — evidence that price history itself is the workhorse feature. Tobek & Hronec (Journal of Financial Markets, 2021) show a neural-net aggregate of 153 published anomalies still earns a value-weighted FF5 alpha of 0.843%/month (t=5.67) internationally and survives in a liquid-stock universe, with US-trained evidence transferring abroad.
- THE COMPLEXITY DEBATE (frontier caution): Kelly, Malamud & Zhou, 'The Virtue of Complexity in Return Prediction' (Journal of Finance, 2024, 79:459-503) claim heavily over-parameterized models (more parameters than observations) predict better out-of-sample. Nagel (2025, 'Seemingly Virtuous Complexity') counters that the apparent gains are largely a mechanical volatility-timing artifact of the random-features implementation and vanish in larger samples; Kelly & Malamud published a 2025 rejoinder ('Understanding the Virtue of Complexity'). Lesson: even top journals are actively fighting over whether ML complexity per se helps — treat any 'bigger model = better returns' claim as unsettled.

### What it means for this project

- Calibrate expectations to the literature: the best academic ML models explain ~0.4% of monthly individual-stock return variance out-of-sample. Edges this small mean your bot's results over months of paper trading are mostly noise; you cannot statistically distinguish skill from luck at $20k over a year, so define success as building correct infrastructure and measurement, not P&L.
- Assume any backtest you like is overfit until proven otherwise: log every configuration/hyperparameter combination you try, then apply Bailey-López de Prado's Deflated Sharpe Ratio or Harvey-Liu's haircut before believing a Sharpe. Rule of thumb from the math: after ~1,000 trials, a backtest Sharpe of ~3 is the EXPECTED value of pure noise.
- Use López de Prado's validation hygiene: walk-forward or purged K-fold cross-validation with an embargo gap between train and test sets (financial labels overlap in time, so vanilla scikit-learn CV leaks and inflates accuracy); fix your test set once and touch it rarely.
- Discount every published anomaly or blog-post strategy by at least the McLean-Pontiff numbers (26% weaker out-of-sample, 58% weaker post-publication), and remember Chen-Velikov: the average published anomaly nets ~4 bps/month after realistic spreads — for a $20k account that is about $10/year, i.e., below your data-subscription cost.
- Model transaction costs from day one, pessimistically: use effective (not quoted) spreads, add slippage, and penalize turnover. Avramov et al. show ML alpha concentrates in microcaps and distressed names where a retail bot's fills would be far worse than the backtest assumes; restrict your universe to liquid stocks and accept that measured alpha will shrink — that shrinkage is the honest number.
- Spend your feature-engineering budget where the literature finds signal: momentum (12-1 month), short-term reversal (1 month), industry momentum, volatility, and liquidity measures — all computable from free daily OHLCV data. Exotic alternative data is not where academic ML gets its power; price history is.
- Prefer tree ensembles (gradient boosting, random forests) or shallow neural nets over deep architectures: GKX found shallow nets beat deep ones on this data, and simpler models are harder to overfit with your sample sizes. Always run a linear/OLS baseline and buy-and-hold benchmark; require the ML model to beat both out-of-sample NET of modeled costs before trusting it.
- Treat paper-trading results as an upper bound: paper fills ignore market impact, queue position, and adverse selection. The Fischer-Krauss LSTM lesson applies directly — a strategy can be genuinely predictive in-sample and still net roughly zero after costs in the current regime.
- Do not extrapolate from Medallion: its ~66% gross returns are documented but rest on capacity-capped (~$10B), short-horizon, infrastructure-heavy trading that Renaissance itself could not scale into its public funds (which lost billions). Meanwhile dedicated AI funds with PhD teams (Sentient, Rogers AI ETF) shut down within two years. The realistic ceiling for an individual is learning-grade, not Medallion-grade.
- Read as the core curriculum (all free versions exist): Gu-Kelly-Xiu 2020 (Dacheng Xiu hosts the PDF), Bailey et al. 2014 in AMS Notices, López de Prado's '10 Reasons' GARP whitepaper, McLean-Pontiff 2016, Harvey-Liu-Zhu 2016, Hou-Xue-Zhang 2020, and Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen 2023 with its open-source GitHub data — together they are the PhD-grade evidence base for everything above.

## Emotions and behavioral finance in trading: prospect theory, the disposition effect, overconfidence, the behavior gap, sensation-seeking, and whether automation/rule-based trading actually helps

### Key findings

- PROSPECT THEORY / LOSS AVERSION: Kahneman & Tversky (1979, Econometrica, 'Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk') showed people evaluate outcomes as gains/losses relative to a reference point, are risk-averse over gains but risk-SEEKING over losses. Tversky & Kahneman (1992, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty, cumulative prospect theory) estimated a loss-aversion coefficient of about 2.25 - a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain pleases. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize largely for this work.
- LOSS AVERSION IN PROFESSIONAL TRADERS: Coval & Shumway (2005, Journal of Finance, 'Do Behavioral Biases Affect Prices?', Smith Breeden Prize winner) studied Chicago Board of Trade proprietary traders and found traders with morning losses were about 16% more likely to take above-average afternoon risk than traders with morning gains - classic 'get back to even' risk escalation, present even in full-time professionals. The price pressure their desperate trades created reverted within about 10 minutes.
- DISPOSITION EFFECT: Named by Shefrin & Statman (1985, Journal of Finance) - the tendency to sell winners too early and ride losers too long. Odean (1998, Journal of Finance, 'Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?'), using ~10,000 discount-brokerage accounts 1987-1993, found investors were roughly 1.5-2x more likely to realize gains than losses, and the behavior was costly: winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept by 3.4 percentage points over the following year. Tax-motivated loss selling appeared only in December; rebalancing and trading costs did not explain it.
- AUTOMATION REMOVES THE DISPOSITION EFFECT (LAB EVIDENCE): Weber & Camerer (1998, Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization) replicated the disposition effect experimentally (60% of shares sold were winners vs 40% losers). Crucially, in a condition where holdings were AUTOMATICALLY sold at the end of each period (forcing a fresh, deliberate re-purchase decision), the disposition effect was greatly reduced - direct experimental evidence that automating exits strips out the bias.
- OVERCONFIDENCE AND TRADING VOLUME: Barber & Odean (2000, Journal of Finance, 'Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth'), 66,465 households at a discount broker 1991-1996: average household turnover was 75%/year; the most active quintile earned 11.4%/yr versus a market return of 17.9% - about 6.5 percentage points of annual underperformance driven mostly by trading costs. Barber & Odean (2001, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 'Boys Will Be Boys'): men traded 45% more than women (single men 67% more than single women); trading reduced men's net returns by 2.65 pp/yr vs 1.72 pp/yr for women - the gender gap is interpreted as an overconfidence effect, since men are documentedly more overconfident in finance.
- THE BEHAVIOR GAP: Morningstar's 'Mind the Gap 2024' study found the average dollar invested in US funds earned ~1.1 percentage points per year LESS than the funds' own time-weighted returns over the 10 years to end-2023 (~6.3% vs 7.3%) - investors captured only about 85% of their funds' returns because of mistimed buys and sells, with the gap largest in the most volatile funds. (The widely quoted DALBAR figures showing much larger gaps use a criticized methodology; Morningstar's is the more defensible academic-grade estimate.) Related: Chang, Solomon & Westerfield (2016, Journal of Finance, 'Looking for Someone to Blame') found the disposition effect exists only for self-directed assets; for delegated assets (mutual funds) it REVERSES, consistent with cognitive dissonance - people can't fire themselves, but they can blame a manager or a system.
- SENSATION-SEEKING AND GAMBLING MOTIVES: Grinblatt & Keloharju (2009, Journal of Finance, 'Sensation Seeking, Overconfidence, and Trading Activity') linked Finnish trading records to drivers' speeding-ticket records and mandatory military psychological profiles: both sensation-seekers (more speeding tickets) and overconfident individuals trade significantly more frequently. Dorn & Sengmueller (2009, Management Science, 'Trading as Entertainment?'), ~1,000 German brokerage clients: those who report enjoying trading or gambling turn over their portfolios at TWICE the rate of peers; entertainment motives more than doubled the explainable variation in turnover. Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean's Taiwan day-trading studies (e.g., 'The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill', Journal of Financial Economics 2014) found fewer than 1% of day traders are predictably profitable net of fees; aggregate day-trader performance was reliably negative in 14 of 15 years studied.
- EMOTIONAL REACTIVITY PREDICTS WORSE TRADING: Lo, Repin & Steenbarger (2005, American Economic Review, 'Fear and Greed in Financial Markets: A Clinical Study of Day-Traders') tracked 80 traders' daily emotional states against their P&L: subjects with more intense emotional reactions to gains AND losses had significantly worse trading performance. Notably, personality inventories revealed no innate 'trader personality' - suggesting emotional discipline is trainable/engineerable rather than inborn.
- RULES BEAT JUDGMENT GENERALLY: Grove, Zald, Lebow, Snitz & Nelson (2000, Psychological Assessment) meta-analyzed 136 studies of clinical vs mechanical prediction across domains: simple mechanical/statistical rules were on average ~10% more accurate than expert human judgment, substantially better in 33-47% of studies, and substantially worse in only 6-16% - regardless of the judge's experience. This is the foundational evidence base for rule-based over discretionary decision-making.
- PEOPLE ABANDON ALGORITHMS AFTER SEEING THEM ERR: Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 'Algorithm Aversion') showed that once people watch an algorithm make mistakes, they abandon it in favor of their own (worse) judgment - even when the algorithm demonstrably outperforms them. Their follow-up (2018, Management Science, 'Overcoming Algorithm Aversion') found people WILL stick with an imperfect algorithm if given even a small ability to modify it. This is the single most relevant psychology finding for anyone running a trading bot: the predicted failure mode is overriding or abandoning the system after its first drawdown.
- SYSTEMATIC VS DISCRETIONARY FUNDS: Harvey, Rattray, Sinclair & van Hemert (2017, Journal of Portfolio Management, 'Man vs. Machine', Bernstein Fabozzi Jacobs Levy Award winner) compared systematic (rules-based) and discretionary hedge funds 1996-2014: after adjusting for volatility and factor exposures, risk-adjusted performance (appraisal ratios) was similar - systematic funds were NOT inferior, and the authors conclude allocators' wariness of them is unjustified 'algorithm aversion'. Automation doesn't create alpha by itself, but it doesn't cost you anything and removes the human behavioral drag.
- EVEN INSTITUTIONS ABANDON STRATEGIES AT THE WRONG TIME: Goyal & Wahal (2008, Journal of Finance), studying ~3,400 pension plan sponsors 1994-2003: sponsors hire managers after big outperformance and fire them after underperformance, but post-firing the fired managers do as well as (sometimes better than) their replacements - the round-trip switching added nothing. Professional committees exhibit the same chase-and-capitulate cycle as retail investors, which is why pre-committed evaluation rules matter.

### What it means for this project

- Your biggest documented edge as an amateur is not signal quality - it is removing yourself from the execution loop. The behavioral drag documented in this literature (disposition effect ~3.4 pp on round trips, overtrading ~2-6.5 pp/yr, timing gap ~1.1 pp/yr) is roughly the size of the entire edge most retail strategies claim. A bot that merely prevents these mistakes is doing real, measurable work even with a mediocre signal.
- Automate exits, not just entries. Weber & Camerer showed the disposition effect largely disappears when selling is automatic. Hard-code stop-losses and profit-taking rules into the bot; never leave the 'when to sell a loser' decision to your in-the-moment self, because Odean's data says that self will hold losers and dump winners at a ~3.4 pp/yr cost.
- Write down, before going live, the exact conditions under which you may turn the bot off or modify it (e.g., 'drawdown exceeds 1.5x the worst backtest drawdown' or 'after the pre-registered 6-month evaluation window'). Dietvorst et al. predict you will want to abandon the system right after its first visible errors - which is statistically the worst moment, per Goyal & Wahal's evidence that even pension committees fire strategies at their low point.
- Keep a manual-override log. Every time you intervene in the bot's decisions, record it and compute the P&L of the override vs what the bot would have done. Grove et al.'s meta-analysis (rules beat judgment ~10% on average) predicts your overrides will net out negative; seeing your own data on this is the cheapest tuition available, and paper trading makes it free.
- If you must scratch the discretionary itch, use Dietvorst's 2018 finding: give yourself one small, bounded knob (e.g., a position-size multiplier within 0.5x-1.5x) rather than veto power over trades. Bounded modification keeps people using the algorithm; unlimited veto power leads to abandonment.
- Build a daily loss limit INTO THE CODE, not into your willpower. Coval & Shumway showed even professional CBOT traders take ~16% more risk after morning losses. 'Revenge trading' after a losing streak is the single most reliably documented emotional failure - have the bot halt for the day/week at a preset loss threshold.
- Minimize trade frequency. Barber & Odean's 6.5 pp/yr underperformance for the most active traders was mostly costs and bad selection. When you eventually move from paper to real money, every extra round trip must clear commissions, spread, and slippage; a backtest that ignores these will flatter high-turnover strategies most.
- Be honest about the entertainment motive. Dorn & Sengmueller show people who trade for fun trade twice as much; Grinblatt & Keloharju show sensation-seekers trade more. An educational paper-trading project is exactly the right container for this drive - if it stays fun-driven when real money arrives, cap the 'fun' account at money you can lose (and note that fewer than 1% of day traders are predictably profitable net of fees in the best available data).
- Measure your own behavior gap: track the bot's time-weighted return vs your actual dollar-weighted return including every time you paused it, resized it, or restarted it. If the second number is lower, the gap is the empirical cost of your interventions - the same 1.1 pp/yr phenomenon Morningstar measures in fund investors.
- Calibrate expectations: Harvey et al. found systematic funds match, not beat, discretionary ones on risk-adjusted returns. Automation removes behavioral drag; it does not manufacture alpha. Treat the project's success metric as 'did I follow the system and learn' rather than 'did I beat SPY in six months' - a horizon over which results are mostly noise. (Also note: this is a summary of published research, not personalized financial advice.)

## How institutions actually trade and why their edge does not transfer to a $20k retail algo account

### Key findings

- RENAISSANCE/MEDALLION (Zuckerman's reporting): Gregory Zuckerman's 'The Man Who Solved the Market' (2019, Penguin) documents Medallion returning roughly 66% per year gross and ~39% net of fees from 1988-2018, with 5% management and 44% performance fees, and over $100B in cumulative profits. Bradford Cornell ('Medallion Fund: The Ultimate Counterexample', 2020) computes that $100 in Medallion in 1988 would have grown to ~$398.7 million by 2018 — and stresses this is inexplicable under standard asset-pricing theory precisely because the fund is CAPPED: Renaissance holds Medallion at roughly $10B by forcibly distributing profits every year. The strategies are short-horizon statistical signals whose alpha vanishes at scale — the win rate is barely above chance (Zuckerman quotes the figure that Medallion is right on about 50.75% of trades, made profitable only by millions of bets and heavy leverage).
- MEDALLION'S LEVERAGE WAS INSTITUTIONAL-ONLY: The 2014 US Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations report 'Abuse of Structured Financial Products' documents that Renaissance obtained leverage far beyond Reg-T limits (up to ~17x) through 'basket options' sold by Deutsche Bank and Barclays from ~1998-2013 — structures that also recharacterized short-term gains for tax purposes. No retail account can replicate this financing structure; a US retail margin account is capped at 2x overnight (4x intraday).
- CAPACITY IS THE CENTRAL FACT: Renaissance's own outside-investor fund (RIEF), built for tens of billions in capacity with longer holding periods, has returns nowhere near Medallion's — in 2020 Medallion reportedly gained ~76% while RIEF lost ~20% (widely reported, e.g. Bloomberg/FT). Same firm, same PhDs, different capacity constraint. Academic theory formalizes this: Berk & Green (Journal of Political Economy, 2004) show skilled managers rationally scale until net-of-fee alpha is competed to zero; Pastor, Stambaugh & Taylor ('Scale and Skill in Active Management', Journal of Financial Economics 116, 2015, pp. 23-45) find strong empirical evidence of industry-level decreasing returns to scale — skill has risen over time but performance hasn't, because aggregate capital chases the same alphas.
- AQR/TWO SIGMA/DE SHAW PUBLISH THEIR SIGNALS BECAUSE SIGNALS ARE NOT THE EDGE: AQR's principals publish in top journals — Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen 'Value and Momentum Everywhere' (Journal of Finance, 2013); Frazzini & Pedersen 'Betting Against Beta' (JFE, 2014); Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen 'Buffett's Alpha' (Financial Analysts Journal 74(4), 2018), which shows even Warren Buffett's record decomposes into cheap ~1.7x leverage from insurance float applied to boring low-beta/quality factor exposure, with alpha statistically insignificant after controlling for BAB and QMJ factors. The proprietary part is implementation: Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz ('Trading Costs of Asset Pricing Anomalies', using ~$1 trillion of AQR's own live executions across 19 markets, 1998-2011; published version 'Trading Costs', JFE 2018/2020) show a patient institution's realized costs are less than one-tenth of academic estimates, giving factor strategies an order of magnitude more capacity — but also show short-term reversal (the closest style to most retail bots) is the MOST capacity-constrained by trading costs.
- HFT ECONOMICS ARE WINNER-TAKE-ALL (Budish et al.): Budish, Cramton & Shim ('The High-Frequency Trading Arms Race', Quarterly Journal of Economics 130(4), 2015, pp. 1547-1621) show the ES-SPY arbitrage duration fell from ~97ms (2005) to ~7ms (2011) while per-race profit stayed roughly constant — competition raises speed, not lower rents, creating a socially wasteful arms race under continuous limit-order-book design. The follow-up, Aquilina, Budish & O'Neill ('Quantifying the High-Frequency Trading Arms Race', QJE 137(1), 2022, pp. 493-564, using FCA message data on the London Stock Exchange), measures: ~1 latency race per minute per FTSE-100 symbol; modal race duration 5-10 MICROSECONDS; races account for ~20% of volume; the top 6 firms win over 80% of races; and the 'latency arbitrage tax' is ~0.4-0.5 basis points of volume (~$5B/yr globally in equities), ~17% of the cost of liquidity. A retail bot's round-trip latency (tens to hundreds of milliseconds through a broker API) is 4-6 orders of magnitude too slow to ever participate; this entire profit pool is structurally closed. Baron, Brogaard, Hagströmer & Kirilenko ('Risk and Return in High-Frequency Trading', Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 2019) confirm HFT revenues concentrate persistently in a handful of fastest firms (winner-take-all), and Virtu's IPO filing disclosed 1 losing day in 1,238 trading days (2009-2013) — profitability from market-making scale and speed, not directional prediction.
- INSTITUTIONAL COST ADVANTAGES ARE CONCRETE AND MEASURABLE: (a) Borrow: Drechsler & Drechsler ('The Shorting Premium and Asset Pricing Anomalies', NBER w20282) show the cheap-minus-expensive-to-short portfolio earns 1.31%/month gross, and that most famous anomalies (momentum, idiosyncratic volatility, net issuance, distress) effectively DISAPPEAR in the ~80% of stocks with low borrow fees — the paper profits live in hard-to-borrow names where retail borrow costs are punitive or shares are unavailable (see also Muravyev et al., 'Anomalies and Their Short-Sale Costs', Journal of Finance, 2025). (b) Execution: institutions internalize, cross, and work orders algorithmically over days (Frazzini-Israel-Moskowitz). (c) Data/infrastructure: colocation, direct exchange feeds, and alternative data cost millions per year. One genuine retail counterpoint: Schwarz, Barber, Huang, Jorion & Odean ('The Actual Retail Price of Equity Trades', Journal of Finance, 2025; 85,000 simultaneous real orders) find small retail marketable orders get wholesaler price improvement, but round-trip costs still varied 0.07% to 0.46% across brokers for IDENTICAL trades — broker choice alone moves costs by more than most signals' expected edge.
- PUBLISHED EDGES DECAY: McLean & Pontiff ('Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?', Journal of Finance, 2016) show anomaly returns are ~26% lower out-of-sample and ~58% lower post-publication. Hou, Xue & Zhang ('Replicating Anomalies', Review of Financial Studies, 2020) find that with microcaps de-emphasized (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighting), 65% of 452 published anomalies fail even a t>1.96 hurdle (82% fail at t>2.78), and 96% of the 'trading frictions' category (which includes short-term reversal/liquidity signals) fails. Translation: whatever cross-sectional predictability survives lives disproportionately in microcaps and hard-to-trade corners — exactly where institutions cannot deploy size, but also where spreads are widest.
- WHAT ACADEMICS SAY RETAIL CAN DO: (1) Liquidity provision in small/stressed corners: Nagel ('Evaporating Liquidity', Review of Financial Studies 25(7), 2012) shows short-term reversal returns are compensation for liquidity provision and are strongly predictable by VIX — the expected return to patient limit-order liquidity supply spikes exactly when institutional intermediaries pull back. Barrot, Kaniel & Sraer ('Are retail traders compensated for providing liquidity?', JFE, 2016) show a portfolio mimicking aggregate retail limit-order liquidity provision earned ~19% annualized four-factor alpha (2002-2010), up to ~40% in high-uncertainty periods — BUT actual retail investors failed to capture it because they exited at the wrong times. (2) Patience without career risk: Shleifer & Vishny ('The Limits of Arbitrage', Journal of Finance, 1997) — institutions face redemptions and mandate constraints at exactly the wrong moments; own-capital investors do not. (3) Small capacity as an advantage: at $20k, market impact is ~zero and niches worth $1-5M/year of capacity are economically invisible to any fund (Berk-Green logic). (4) The honest null result: Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean's complete Taiwan trading-record studies (1992-2006; 'The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill', Journal of Financial Markets, 2014; 'Do Day Traders Rationally Learn About Their Ability?') find day traders in aggregate lost ~24 bps per DAY net of fees, aggregate losses in 14 of 15 years, and fewer than 1% of day traders predictably and reliably earned positive abnormal net returns. For short-horizon directional trading, the peer-reviewed answer to 'what can retail do that institutions cannot' is essentially: nothing.

### What it means for this project

- Do not build anything latency-sensitive. The races institutions fight last 5-10 microseconds and the top 6 firms win 80%+ of them (Aquilina-Budish-O'Neill 2022). A broker-API bot is 4-6 orders of magnitude too slow; any backtest edge that depends on being filled at the touch within milliseconds is fictional for you.
- Treat Medallion as evidence about capacity, not a target. Its 66% gross returns exist only because the fund is capped near $10B, distributes profits, uses ~17x bank-provided leverage, and takes millions of tiny bets at ~50.75% win rates with world-class infrastructure. Nothing about it scales DOWN to a $20k account either — the lesson is that the best returns in history came from strategies deliberately kept small.
- Haircut every backtest of a published or published-adjacent signal by 26-58% (McLean-Pontiff 2016) before costs, and assume most anomalies you read about fail replication entirely outside microcaps (Hou-Xue-Zhang 2020). A paper-trading bot is the right vehicle to measure this decay yourself — that IS the education.
- Avoid the short side. Anomaly profits concentrate in expensive-to-short stocks (Drechsler & Drechsler; Muravyev et al. 2025), and retail borrow fees/availability on exactly those names are prohibitive. Long-only or long-tilted implementations are the honest retail version of any factor idea.
- Your one structural cost advantage is zero market impact. Frazzini-Israel-Moskowitz show institutions' binding constraint is price impact at scale; at $20k yours is spread + broker execution. Consequence: (a) broker/execution choice matters enormously — identical retail orders cost 0.07% vs 0.46% round-trip at different brokers (Schwarz et al., JF 2025); measure your paper fills against realistic spreads, especially in small caps; (b) use limit orders, never market orders, in anything illiquid.
- If a durable retail-shaped edge exists in the literature, it is patient liquidity provision in small, stressed corners: short-term reversal as compensation for liquidity supply, with expected returns spiking when VIX is high (Nagel 2012; Barrot-Kaniel-Sraer 2016 found ~19-40% annualized alpha to mimicking retail limit-order flow). But note the same papers show real retail investors failed to capture it due to undisciplined exits — a rules-based bot with strict holding-period discipline is, interestingly, the right form factor to even attempt this.
- Exploit what institutions structurally cannot: hold through drawdowns (no redemptions — Shleifer-Vishny 1997), trade niches with $1-5M capacity that no fund will touch, and simply decline to trade when conditions are bad. Frequency of trading is your enemy: the Taiwan evidence (Barber-Lee-Liu-Odean) shows aggregate day-trading losses of ~24 bps/day and <1% reliably profitable — so benchmark your bot against buy-and-hold after all costs, and treat 'the bot correctly chose not to trade' as a success metric.
- Set expectations from the base rates: a realistic good outcome for a rigorous retail systematic strategy is a modest Sharpe (<1) long-horizon factor tilt, not Medallion. If your paper-trading results show a Sharpe above ~1.5-2 on daily equity signals, the most likely explanations in order are: look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, unrealistic fills, ignored borrow costs, then skill.

## Documented outcomes of retail algorithmic and systematic traders: academic studies, platform post-mortems, prop-firm statistics, and regulator data

### Key findings

- DAY-TRADER BASE RATES (TAIWAN, 15 YEARS OF COMPLETE MARKET DATA): Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, 'The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill: Evidence from Day Trading' (Journal of Financial Markets, 2014; data 1992-2006, every trade on the Taiwan Stock Exchange) find that fewer than 1% of day traders predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees. The top 500 ranked traders subsequently earned +37.9 bps/day after fees, while the bottom-ranked earned -28.9 bps/day after fees — skill exists but is extraordinarily rare and concentrated.
- TRADERS DO NOT RATIONALLY LEARN: Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, 'Do Day Traders Rationally Learn About Their Ability?' (working paper, UC Berkeley/UC Davis) show ~40% of Taiwanese day traders quit within one month, ~80% within two years, only ~7% remain after five years — and nearly three-quarters of all day-trading volume comes from traders with a history of losses. The authors conclude 'trading to learn' is no more rational than playing roulette to learn.
- BRAZIL FUTURES DAY TRADERS: Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, 'Day Trading for a Living?' (SSRN 3423101, 2019/2020; all individuals who began day-trading Brazilian equity-index futures 2013-2015): 97% of those who persisted more than 300 trading days lost money; only 1.1% earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage; only 0.5% earned more than a bank teller's starting salary. The single best trader earned ~US$310/day with a daily standard deviation of US$2,560. No evidence of learning-by-doing.
- ROBINHOOD-ERA RETAIL: Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz, 'Attention-Induced Trading and Returns: Evidence from Robinhood Users' (Journal of Finance 77(6), 2022, pp. 3141-3190): Robinhood users herd into attention-grabbing stocks (app design — top-mover lists, notifications — is partly causal, shown via outage natural experiments); the top stocks bought each day earned average 20-day abnormal returns of -4.7%. Intense retail buying is a contrarian signal.
- RETAIL OPTIONS (PANDEMIC ERA): Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, 'Retail Trading in Options and the Rise of the Big Three Wholesalers' (Journal of Finance 78(6), 2023): the aggregate retail options portfolio lost $2.1B from Nov 2019 to Jun 2021, and aggregate trading costs (distance to midquote) were $6.4B — i.e., most losses came from transaction costs, not bad directional views. Retail prefers cheap weekly options with average bid-ask spreads of 12.6%.
- QUANTOPIAN POST-MORTEM: Quantopian (founded 2011) reached ~300,000+ registered users (widely reported figures range up to 500k accounts) who ran 12M+ backtests; its crowdsourced hedge fund (backed by a $250M Steve Cohen allocation) underperformed, the fund wound down, and the platform shut in October 2020 with ~2 weeks' notice; the team was acqui-hired by Robinhood. Only a tiny fraction of community strategies ever received real allocations, and those that did failed to sustain live performance.
- QUANTOPIAN'S OWN DATA ON BACKTEST OVERFITTING: Wiecki, Campbell, Lent & Stauth, 'All That Glitters Is Not Gold' (Journal of Investing 25(3):69, 2016; SSRN 2745220): across 888 real user algorithms with >=6 months of true out-of-sample data, backtest Sharpe ratio had essentially NO predictive power for out-of-sample performance (R-squared < 0.025). The more backtests a user ran on a strategy, the larger the in-sample/out-of-sample gap (direct empirical evidence of overfitting). A machine-learning model on backtest-behavior features reached only R-squared ~0.17.
- PROP-FIRM CHALLENGE ECONOMICS: FPFX Technologies' analysis of 300,000+ accounts from ~100,000 traders across ~10 prop firms found ~14% ever passed a challenge, ~7% of all traders ever received any payout, and the average payout was ~4% of the nominal funded account size (~$4k on a '$100k' account); traders spent ~$800 on fees over ~3 attempts on average, and ~half of funded traders lost the account within 90 days. FTMO has historically disclosed ~9-10% pass rates; Topstep disclosed 16.8% of Trading Combines started in 2025 were completed. (Firm disclosures and vendor data, not peer-reviewed — but the most direct documentation available.)
- REGULATOR DATA — EUROPE: ESMA's 2018 product-intervention analysis (with national regulators) found 74-89% of retail CFD accounts lose money, average losses per client EUR 1,600-29,000; it banned binary options and capped CFD leverage for retail. The French AMF's October 2014 study of 14,799 retail forex/CFD clients (2009-2013) found 89% lost money over four years with an average loss of ~EUR 10,900 — losses grew with activity ('more you trade, more you lose').
- REGULATOR DATA — US, PRE-INTERNET-BUBBLE: NASAA's 1999 Day Trading Project Group report (consultant Ronald L. Johnson's account-level audit at All-Tech Investment Group) found ~70% of day-trading customers lost money and only 11.5% of sampled accounts showed the ability to trade profitably short-term; in one Massachusetts branch 67 of 68 accounts lost money. Numbers strikingly consistent with 2019 Brazil and 1992-2006 Taiwan data — the failure base rate is stable across decades, countries, and technology regimes.
- COPY/SOCIAL TRADING: Apesteguia, Oechssler & Weidenholzer, 'Copy Trading' (Management Science 66(12), 2020) show experimentally that the option to copy top-ranked traders significantly INCREASES follower risk-taking without improving expected returns. Krafft et al. (arXiv 1406.7729, eToro data) show popularity dynamics are driven by popularity-times-perceived-quality feedback, not pure skill — followed traders are 'often but not always' the most successful. Pelster & Hofmann (Journal of Banking & Finance, 2018) document that eToro signal-providers being watched by followers exhibit a STRONGER disposition effect (holding losers longer). A TalTech thesis on 28 eToro copy-portfolios (2017-2020) found 21 of 28 with positive CAPM alpha, but the sample is tiny, survivorship-biased, and pre-2020. Meanwhile eToro's own ESMA-mandated disclosure has run at roughly 74-76% of retail CFD accounts losing money.
- COLLECTIVE2: no peer-reviewed survival study of Collective2 strategies exists (verified by search); evidence is anecdotal (e.g., a vendor reporting 80-95% subscriber churn even on profitable low-drawdown systems). The nearest rigorous analogue is the Quantopian 888-algorithm study plus hedge-fund attrition literature (cumulative fund failure rates of ~32-66% within 7 years). Treat any Collective2 leaderboard as an extreme survivorship-bias environment.
- DOCUMENTED INDIVIDUAL SUCCESSES (THE RARE TAIL): (1) Rob Carver — ex-AHL (Man Group) portfolio manager, author of 'Systematic Trading' (2015) — has published his personal diversified-futures system results on his blog (qoppac) annually since 2014; recent example: +23.7% Apr 2025-Apr 2026, with full methodology disclosed; his edge profile is institutional training + diversification + cost control, targeting modest volatility, not get-rich returns. (2) Kevin Davey won/placed 1st-2nd in the audited Robbins World Cup Championship of Futures Trading three consecutive years with algorithmic systems (+148% in 2005, +107% in 2006, +112% in 2007) and documented his full process in 'Building Winning Algorithmic Trading Systems' (Wiley, 2014) — but contest returns are achieved on small accounts with extreme risk and are explicitly not sustainable portfolio returns. Both cases involve decades of prior professional experience.
- SYNTHESIS ACROSS ALL SOURCES: every dataset with complete (non-self-selected) records — Taiwan, Brazil, France, EU CFD accounts, US day-trading audits — converges on 70-97% of active retail speculators losing money, with the reliably profitable fraction between ~0.5% and ~1%; the main destroyers of returns are transaction costs, overtrading, leverage, and attention-driven stock selection, while the main destroyer of ALGORITHMIC retail returns specifically is backtest overfitting (Quantopian's R-squared < 0.025 result).

### What it means for this project

- Paper trading a $20k educational bot is precisely the correct posture: the documented base rate of reliably profitable retail speculators is ~0.5-1% (Taiwan, Brazil), so treat the project's expected monetary value as zero and its expected educational value as the real return. Any plan that depends on the bot producing income contradicts every complete dataset ever studied.
- Do not trust your backtest Sharpe ratio — at all. Quantopian's own 888-algorithm study found backtest Sharpe predicts out-of-sample Sharpe with R-squared < 0.025. Practical protocol: lock a true out-of-sample holdout period you never touch, use walk-forward testing, log how many backtest iterations each strategy has consumed (the Quantopian data shows in/out-of-sample gap GROWS with number of backtests), and prefer strategy-selection criteria based on drawdown, volatility, and turnover, which did show predictive value.
- Model costs obsessively. The retail options study shows most retail losses were transaction costs ($6.4B in costs vs $2.1B net loss), not bad predictions. In the bot, charge yourself realistic IBKR commissions plus conservative slippage (at least the half-spread) on every simulated fill, and reject any strategy whose edge is smaller than ~2x modeled costs. Avoid wide-spread instruments (weekly options averaged 12.6% bid-ask spreads).
- Treat crowd attention as a contrarian or at best a risk signal: the top Robinhood-herded stocks earned -4.7% abnormal returns over the next 20 days. Never let the bot buy simply because a ticker is trending, gapping, or on a most-active list.
- Skip prop-firm challenges as a capital route: documented funnel is ~10-16% pass a challenge, ~7% of all entrants ever receive a payout, and the average payout is ~4% of the nominal account (~$4k on '$100k') against ~$800 average cumulative fees — a negative-expected-value lottery for most participants.
- Avoid leveraged CFD/forex instruments entirely: regulators (ESMA 2018, AMF 2014) documented 74-89% loss rates and average four-year losses around EUR 10,900, and the AMF found losses INCREASE with trading activity and experience.
- Do not outsource to copy trading as a shortcut: popularity on platforms like eToro is only loosely tied to skill, copying causally increases risk-taking (Management Science 2020 experiment), and leaders being watched hold losers longer (disposition effect). Platform leaderboards (eToro, Collective2) are severe survivorship-bias environments.
- Pre-commit to kill criteria before any live deployment: the Taiwan learning study shows the modal failure mode is persisting through losses for years (three-quarters of day-trading volume comes from traders with losing histories). Write down, in advance, the drawdown, sample size, and evaluation horizon at which each strategy — and the whole project — gets shut off, so the decision is made by the rules, not by sunk-cost psychology.
- Calibrate 'success' against documented successes, not marketing: Rob Carver (ex-AHL professional, fully disclosed methodology, diversified futures, volatility-targeted, ~20% in a good year) is what sustainable individual systematic trading looks like; Kevin Davey's audited +100%/yr figures are small-account contest returns with extreme risk. If a course, prop firm, or YouTube figure implies triple-digit sustainable returns are normal, the Brazil study's punchline applies: the single best day trader out of ~20,000 made ~$310/day with a $2,560 daily standard deviation.
- Learn the meta-lesson of Quantopian: 500k users, 12M backtests, institutional capital, and professional vetting still could not extract deployable alpha from crowdsourced retail strategies. A solo developer should assume their prior is strictly worse — which is fine for an educational project, and exactly why the deliverable should be infrastructure skill, statistical hygiene (deflated Sharpe, walk-forward, cost modeling), and a documented research journal rather than P&L.
- If the project ever goes live, size it as tuition: a small fraction of the $20k, in liquid low-spread instruments (major-index ETFs/futures, not options or CFDs), with position sizes such that a full strategy failure costs less than a university course.

## AI and LLM-based trading: measured results vs hype — peer-reviewed evidence on LLM sentiment strategies, financial LLMs (BloombergGPT/FinGPT), real-money AI funds, the backtest-to-live gap, and lookahead contamination

### Key findings

- Lopez-Lira & Tang (U. Florida), 'Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements?' (arXiv 2304.07619, first posted 2023; published in the Journal of Financial Economics, 2026) is the landmark study: GPT-4 scoring news headlines as good/bad for a stock produced long-short backtest Sharpe ratios of roughly 3.1 (GPT-3.5) to 3.8 (GPT-4) before transaction costs, with ~90% portfolio-day 'hit rates'. Critically, the sample was deliberately restricted to Oct 2021 onward — after the model's Sept 2021 knowledge cutoff — precisely to avoid lookahead bias.
- The same paper's fine print undercuts the headline numbers: the ~90% hit rate applies to the initial overnight market reaction, which is NOT tradable (the price has already moved by the time you can act); the tradable signal is the subsequent drift, which is concentrated in small, illiquid stocks and negative news; returns diminish sharply net of transaction costs; and the authors document that predictability DECLINED after ChatGPT's public release in Nov 2022 — consistent with crowding as many market participants began running the same models on the same headlines.
- Glasserman & Lin (Columbia), 'Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis' (arXiv 2309.17322 / SSRN 4586726, 2023) tested contamination directly by anonymizing company names in headlines. In-sample (inside GPT's training window), anonymized headlines actually OUTPERFORMED original ones — showing both that memorized company knowledge distorts predictions (a 'distraction effect') and that naive in-training-window backtests are unreliable. They recommend entity-anonymized backtesting.
- Sarkar (Harvard) & Vafa (Columbia/Harvard), 'Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models' (SSRN 4754678; ICML) produced the cleanest smoking gun for contamination: when Llama 2 was prompted to identify risks from Sept–Nov 2019 earnings calls, it mentioned Covid-19 in over 25% of cases — the model 'predicted' the future because the future was in its training data. They also show prompting the model to 'pretend it is 2019' does NOT reliably remove the bias.
- Ludwig, Mullainathan & Rambachan, 'Large Language Models: An Applied Econometric Framework' (NBER Working Paper 33344, 2024/25; Annual Review of Economics) formalizes the rule: LLM-based forecasting research is only valid under 'no training leakage' between the LLM's training corpus and the evaluation sample. Absent that, results are econometrically uninterpretable — seemingly innocuous choices of model or prompt can produce dramatically different estimates.
- Gao, Jiang & Yan (CUHK), 'A Test of Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts' (arXiv 2512.23847, 2025) built a statistical detector ('Lookahead Propensity'): LLM headline-based return forecasts are significantly MORE accurate exactly on the firm-dates the model can recall from training, and this amplification vanishes on post-training-cutoff data — direct quantitative evidence that part of published LLM 'alpha' is memorization, not forecasting skill.
- BloombergGPT (Wu et al. 2023, arXiv 2303.17564, 50B parameters trained on Bloomberg's proprietary corpus) demonstrated only financial NLP benchmark gains — sentiment classification, named-entity recognition, ConvFinQA question answering (~43% exact match) — and published zero trading performance. It was never released publicly. FinGPT (Yang, Liu et al. 2023, arXiv 2306.06031, open source) is a LoRA fine-tuning pipeline that beat BloombergGPT on some sentiment benchmarks cheaply; its trading claims are cost-free backtests, not audited live results. Neither paper demonstrates that a financial LLM makes money.
- Real-money evidence is sobering. AIEQ (the 'AI Powered Equity ETF' using IBM Watson, launched Oct 2017) has returned roughly 7% annualized vs ~12% for the S&P 500 since inception, charges 0.83% fees (~8x SPY), and per New Constructs analysis drifted into closet indexing (0.91 correlation with S&P 500) after its original strategy lagged.
- AI hedge funds as a class have not beaten the index: the Eurekahedge AI Hedge Fund Index returned ~115% cumulative Jan 2011–Jan 2020 vs ~210% for the S&P 500, and ~9.8% annualized Dec 2009–Jul 2024 vs 13.7% for the S&P 500 (IG/Eurekahedge data). Index composition matters — Preqin's broader AI-fund universe showed different numbers over 2016–2019 — but no dataset shows AI funds reliably beating buy-and-hold US equities.
- Chen & Ren, 'Do AI-powered mutual funds perform better?' (Finance Research Letters, 2022) studied 15 AI-driven funds (Nov 2017–Dec 2019): they did NOT beat the market (statistically indistinguishable in 25 of 26 months), though they beat human-managed peers — mostly through lower turnover (31% vs 72%) and hence lower transaction costs, not superior prediction.
- On the backtest-vs-live gap generally: Buczynski, Cuzzolin & Sahakian, 'A review of machine learning experiments in equity investment decision-making' (International Journal of Data Science and Analytics, 2021) reviewed 27 peer-reviewed ML forecasting experiments from 2000–2018 and found the published results systematically fail to translate to live trading — common failures include ignored transaction costs, survivorship bias, unrealistic execution assumptions, and no live deployment at all.
- Follow-up LLM studies show fragile, model-dependent results: Chen, Tang, Zhou & Zhu, 'ChatGPT and DeepSeek: Can They Predict the Stock Market and Macroeconomy?' (arXiv 2502.10008, 2025) found ChatGPT extracted a predictive signal from Wall Street Journal news (driven by investor underreaction to positive news) while DeepSeek did not — the 'edge' is not a general LLM property but varies by model, language, and period. A growing 2026 literature (Look-Ahead-Bench, arXiv 2601.13770; DatedGPT, arXiv 2603.11838) is now building point-in-time LLMs specifically because standard models cannot be trusted for historical backtests.

### What it means for this project

- Never backtest an LLM strategy on dates before the model's training cutoff. If you use GPT-4o/Claude/Llama to score old news, the model may literally remember what happened next (Sarkar & Vafa's Covid result). Restrict evaluation to post-cutoff periods, as Lopez-Lira & Tang did, and record the exact model version and cutoff date in your logs.
- Telling the model 'pretend you don't know anything after 2021' does not work — Sarkar & Vafa showed prompting cannot reliably suppress memorized future knowledge. If you must test on historical data, strip company names and tickers from the text first (Glasserman & Lin's anonymization procedure).
- Discount any Sharpe ratio above ~2 in an LLM-sentiment backtest as almost certainly an artifact. The famous 3.8 Sharpe in Lopez-Lira & Tang was before costs, concentrated in small illiquid stocks, partly in a non-tradable overnight reaction, and decayed after ChatGPT's release. For a $20k account, small-cap spreads and slippage would consume most of the paper alpha — model realistic fills (next open at best), commissions, and spread costs in every backtest.
- Assume the vanilla strategy is crowded: 'GPT reads headlines, buy good news' is now published, replicated, and run by professional desks with lower latency than you. Documented post-2022 decay means you should treat this as a learning exercise in pipeline-building, not an expected source of returns.
- Use LLMs for what they are proven at: parsing and classifying financial text (sentiment labels, entity extraction, summarizing filings) — that is all BloombergGPT and FinGPT actually demonstrated. Treat the LLM as a feature-extraction component in your pipeline, and validate a sample of its labels by hand (Ludwig-Mullainathan-Rambachan's validation-sample rule) before trusting them downstream.
- Benchmark everything against buy-and-hold SPY. Professionally-run AI vehicles — AIEQ (~7% vs ~12% annualized) and the Eurekahedge AI hedge fund index (9.8% vs 13.7%) — lost to the index over long periods despite full-time teams and better data. If your paper-trading bot lags SPY, that is the normal, documented outcome, and beating SPY in one backtest is weak evidence of skill.
- Keep turnover low. The only robust edge Chen & Ren found for AI funds over human funds came from trading less (31% vs 72% turnover), not predicting better. For a small account, cost discipline is the one 'AI fund' lesson that transfers directly.
- Structure the project to measure the backtest-live gap itself: run the identical strategy in backtest and in live paper trading simultaneously for a few months and compare. The divergence you observe (latency, fills, missing data, model drift as providers update the LLM) is the core educational payoff, and it is exactly the failure mode the Buczynski et al. review says published papers omit.
